## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

JSW Steel is India's largest steel producer by capacity (~35-36 MTPA as of FY26), manufacturing hot-rolled coils, cold-rolled coils, galvanised steel, colour-coated steel, TMT bars, plates, and specialty products. The business is a **commodity producer** — it buys iron ore and coking coal, converts them into steel using blast furnaces and electric arc furnaces, and sells the output into a market where pricing is determined by global supply-demand dynamics, not brand loyalty.

Buffett: *"The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow, but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage."*

**The Buffett Classification:** JSW Steel is a **capital-intensive commodity business** — the opposite of what Buffett typically seeks. It has no meaningful pricing power (steel prices are set by global markets and Chinese capacity), no switching costs (steel is fungible), and no brand moat (customers buy on price and specification). It requires massive ongoing capital expenditure to maintain and expand capacity. Buffett has historically avoided commodity businesses, famously regretting his investment in Berkshire Hathaway (a textile mill) and steering clear of steel, airlines, and other capital-intensive cyclicals.

**However**, JSW Steel does possess some structural advantages within the Indian context: it is the lowest-cost producer among Indian primary steelmakers due to its coastal locations (Dolvi near Mumbai port, Vijayanagar with captive iron ore), vertical integration (24 captive iron ore mines), and scale. India's steel consumption per capita (~87 kg) is well below the global average (~230 kg), providing a secular demand tailwind. The company is aggressively expanding from ~36 MTPA to 50 MTPA by FY31 through JVs with JFE Steel (Japan) and POSCO (South Korea).

**Would Buffett understand this business?** Yes — it is simple to understand. Would he invest? Almost certainly not at current valuations. *"I don't understand steel, I don't want to understand steel, and I would rather buy wonderful businesses at fair prices."* The economics are driven by steel price cycles, input costs, and capital allocation discipline — not by durable competitive advantages.

> **EBIT vs. EBITDA — Why This Analysis Uses EBIT:** Screener.in's "Operating Profit" is calculated pre-D&A, making it EBITDA-equivalent. This analysis computes and tracks **EBIT = Operating Profit − D&A** as the true operating profitability metric. Buffett (2000 Letter): *"References to EBITDA make us shudder — does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?"* Munger: *"I think every time you see the word EBITDA, you should substitute the phrase 'bullshit earnings.'"* EBIT tells you what the business earns after maintaining its asset base — for a capital-intensive steelmaker like JSW Steel where D&A reached ₹9,309 Cr (FY25), this distinction is enormous: EBIT Margin (7.9%) paints a fundamentally different picture than pre-D&A OPM (13%).

> **Screener Data Note (FY16):** The Net Profit row shows consolidated profit (including minority interest share of losses = -₹481 Cr), while EPS is calculated by Screener on profit attributable to parent shareholders only (implying ~-₹330 Cr attributable profit). This creates a visible ~₹151 Cr gap between NP/Shares and reported EPS for FY16. Both figures are raw Screener data and have been retained as-is.

**Trend Narrative:**

This is a textbook commodity cyclical income statement. Revenue has grown at a 12.3% CAGR over 10 years (FY15-FY25) driven by both volume expansion (capacity grew from ~14 MTPA to ~36 MTPA) and steel price cycles. However, profitability is wildly cyclical — pre-D&A OPM has ranged from **11% (FY23) to 27% (FY22)**, and EBIT Margin from **6.6% (FY23) to 22.6% (FY22)**. The gap between OPM and EBIT Margin has remained substantial throughout the analysis period (~6 pp in FY14, ~5 pp in FY25), though in absolute rupee terms D&A has tripled from ₹3,183 Cr to ₹9,309 Cr as the asset base expanded — a pattern that EBITDA-based analysis would entirely miss. EPS has swung from **-₹1.39 (FY16) to ₹85.49 (FY22)**. The FY22 supercycle saw net profit of ₹20,938 Cr; just three years later in FY25, net profit collapsed to ₹3,491 Cr — an **83% decline**.

Earnings growth has NOT outpaced revenue growth on a sustained basis. The 10Y profit CAGR (7.3%) lags the 10Y sales CAGR (12.3%), meaning margins have structurally compressed as the company scaled. Interest costs have nearly tripled (2.8x) from ₹3,048 Cr (FY14) to ₹8,412 Cr (FY25), reflecting the massive debt-funded expansion.

Buffett: *"When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."* Steel's economics are cyclically brilliant and cyclically terrible — and no management can control the cycle.

**Assessment:**

The balance sheet is **NOT** a financial fortress by Buffett standards. Debt/Equity has consistently been above 1.0x — ranging from 1.07 (FY22, the best year) to 2.23 (FY16, the worst). ## 10. COMPETITIVE POSITION & MOAT ANALYSIS

**Moat Type:** Narrow cost advantage — NOT a wide moat.

JSW Steel's competitive advantages are: (1) **India's largest steelmaker by capacity** (~36 MTPA, ahead of Tata Steel's ~21 MTPA domestic and SAIL's ~20 MTPA), (2) **Coastal plant locations** enabling lower logistics costs and raw material import flexibility, (3) **Captive iron ore** from 24 mines in Odisha/Karnataka reducing input cost volatility, (4) **Value-added product mix** (galvanised, colour-coated, automotive-grade steel) at ~55% of revenue, and (5) **Scale-driven cost leadership** through India's largest blast furnace at Vijayanagar.

**Key Competitors:**
- **Tata Steel** — Diversified globally (India + Europe); stronger brand but burdened by European losses
- **SAIL** — Government-owned; lower cost of capital but weaker execution
- **Jindal Steel & Power** — Aggressive competitor in long products
- **ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel** — Global technology with India presence
- **Chinese imports** — THE existential threat; China produces 1,000+ MTPA vs India's ~140 MTPA

**Is the moat enduring?** The cost advantage is real but narrow. It can be replicated by any well-funded competitor building a coastal integrated plant. Chinese overcapacity is a permanent structural threat that caps steel prices globally. India's safeguard duties provide temporary relief, but policy can change.

**Is the business susceptible to change?** Yes — significantly. The steel industry is undergoing a transition toward green steel (hydrogen-based reduction, electric arc furnaces), which could require massive capital investment and may favour new entrants or disruptors. Buffett: *"We're looking for the absence of change to protect ways that are already making a lot of money."* Steel is NOT a business where absence of change can be assumed.

## 21. REGULATORY RISKS & POLICY HEADWINDS

**Key Regulatory Framework:** Ministry of Steel, Ministry of Mines (mining leases), DGTR (trade remedies), SEBI (listed company compliance).

**Historical Impact:** India imposed a 15% export duty on steel in May 2022 — devastating for JSW Steel's profitability (FY23 net profit collapsed 80% YoY). The duty was withdrawn in November 2022. More recently, safeguard duties on certain steel imports (effective 2025) have provided temporary relief from Chinese competition.

**Current Environment:** The regulatory stance is broadly supportive — India's National Steel Policy targets 300 MTPA capacity by 2030, and the government is investing heavily in infrastructure (roads, railways, housing) that drives steel demand. However, trade policy (duties on exports/imports) remains unpredictable and can dramatically swing profitability in a single quarter.

**Buffett's Verdict: (b) Manageable but Fairly Priced.** The regulatory risk is real but the market is fully aware of it. There is no mispricing from regulatory fear.
