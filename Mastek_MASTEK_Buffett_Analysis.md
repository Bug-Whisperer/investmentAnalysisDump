## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

### Business Model

Mastek Ltd, established in 1982, is a mid-cap Indian IT services company focused on **digital engineering, cloud transformation (especially Oracle Cloud), and sector-specific technology services**. The company serves clients primarily in:

- **Government & Public Sector** (UK NHS, HMRC, and other UK government bodies — historically the largest revenue contributor)
- **Healthcare & Life Sciences**
- **Financial Services**
- **Retail & Manufacturing**

**Geographic Mix:** The UK & Europe contribute the majority of revenue (~60-65%), followed by North America (~25-30%) and India/Rest of World (~10%). This UK government dependence is both a strength (large, sticky contracts) and a risk (single-geography concentration).

### Buffett Simplicity Test

Would Buffett understand this business? **Yes — with a caveat.** At its core, Mastek sells brainpower — it deploys skilled engineers to help organisations modernise their IT systems, move to the cloud, and build digital platforms. The revenue model is straightforward: bill hours or deliver projects for a fee. Buffett famously said: *"I try to invest in businesses that are so wonderful that an idiot can run them. Because sooner or later, one will."* IT services businesses are people-dependent, not idiot-proof — but the best ones (like Mastek) build deep domain expertise and long-term client relationships that create meaningful switching costs.

### Economic Engine Classification

Mastek is a **switching-cost business** with elements of a **specialised service franchise**. Once embedded in a client's IT infrastructure (especially government systems where compliance, security clearances, and domain knowledge create high switching barriers), the cost and risk of switching vendors is substantial. The UK government work, in particular, creates a quasi-annuity stream — these contracts run for years with extensions.

**Recent Strategic Win (April 2026):** Mastek secured a two-year £15 million contract with the UK Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), with a possible two-year extension — reinforcing the UK government franchise.

> **Note on Gross Profit / Gross Margin:** Screener.in does not separately report Cost of Goods Sold for IT services companies — the expense breakdown goes directly from Revenue to Operating Expenses to Operating Profit. For IT services, OPM (pre-D&A) serves as the closest proxy for gross margin since there are no material raw materials; the primary costs are employee salaries and subcontracting.

> **Valuation Philosophy — EBIT, Not EBITDA:** This analysis follows a pure Buffett/Munger framework. Screener.in's "Operating Profit" is pre-D&A (EBITDA-equivalent), confirmed by the PBT identity: PBT = OP + Other Income − Interest − Depreciation. We compute **EBIT = Screener OP − D&A** as the true after-capital-charge operating profit. Buffett (2000 Letter): *"References to EBITDA make us shudder — does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?"* Munger: *"I think every time you see the word EBITDA, you should substitute the phrase 'bullshit earnings.'"* EBITDA pretends the capital a business consumes to maintain operations is free. EBIT does not. For valuation, we use **Owner Earnings** (Section 8) and **EBIT-based multiples** — never EBITDA.

**Note on FY16:** Revenue dropped sharply due to the demerger of Majesco (insurance software business) in FY16. This makes pre-FY17 comparisons non-comparable on an absolute basis.

**Note on FY26:** The annual FY26 PBT (₹551 Cr) and NP (₹404 Cr) are sourced from the reported full-year results (Tickertape / BSE filings). However, the FY26 sub-components (OP ~580, Other Income ~42, Interest ~34, Depreciation ~73) are estimated by summing quarterly figures. **The sum of estimated components (580 + 42 − 34 − 73 = ₹515 Cr) falls ~₹36 Cr short of the reported annual PBT of ₹551 Cr.** This gap likely reflects inter-quarter consolidation entries, items not captured in quarterly P&L summaries (such as forex gains/losses), or reclassifications at the annual level. The PBT-to-NP relationship (551 × 0.73 ≈ 404) is internally consistent.

### Trend Narrative

**Revenue trajectory:** Post-demerger, Mastek has delivered impressive revenue growth — a 13% CAGR over 10 years and 26% over 5 years. However, growth has decelerated from the post-COVID boom (60.8% in FY21) to single digits in FY26 (7.1%), reflecting the broader IT services slowdown and cautious enterprise spending.

**Note on FY15–FY17 (EBIT < Net Income):** In these years, core operating profits (EBIT) were extremely thin — ₹10 Cr, ₹2 Cr, and ₹30 Cr respectively — and reported Net Income was substantially sustained by non-operating Other Income (treasury income, investment gains). In FY16, EBIT was just ₹2 Cr while NI was ₹14 Cr — virtually all earnings came from non-operating sources. This context is important: the post-demerger business took until FY18 to generate meaningful core operating profits. Pre-FY18 profitability metrics should be treated as non-representative of the current business.

**Margin story:** OPM (pre-D&A) peaked at 21% in FY21-FY22, then compressed to 15-16% by FY25-FY26. **EBIT Margin** tells a slightly different story — it peaked at 19.2% (FY22) and has normalised to ~13.6-13.7% (FY25-FY26). The gap between OPM and EBIT Margin widened slightly from ~1.8pp (FY22: 21% vs 19.2%) to ~2.4pp (FY25: 16% vs 13.6%) as D&A rose post-Evosys, but has since stabilised at ~2-2.5pp. Both margins are compressing, confirming that the business is growing revenue but not translating it into proportionate profit growth. Earnings growth of 7.4% in FY26 on 7.1% revenue growth shows zero operating leverage at current margins.

**EPS growth:** 10-year EPS CAGR of ~31.5% (FY15→FY25) is outstanding, but this includes recovery from the FY15-FY16 trough. The more relevant 5-year EPS CAGR is ~22% — still strong. However, the last 3-year EPS CAGR has slowed to ~7% (FY22→FY25), and FY26 shows only ~7% growth. (Note: Screener.in reports "3Y Compounded Profit Growth: 9%" which uses a NP CAGR methodology that may differ from the EPS CAGR due to share count changes.)

Buffett: *"The best business to own is one that over an extended period can employ large amounts of incremental capital at very high rates of return."* Mastek's recent margin compression suggests the incremental returns on new revenue are declining — a concern for the scaling thesis.

> **Fair P/B derivation (Gordon Growth Model):** Fair P/B = ROE / (CoE − g) = 15.5% / (10% − 4%) = 2.58x, conservatively rounded to 2.5x. Here, g = 4% is the **terminal perpetuity growth rate** (not the near-term BV compounding rate). The formula is valid because g (4%) < CoE (10%). Note: the near-term BV compounding rate of ROE × Retention = 15.5% × 80% = 12.4% is a different concept — it describes how fast book value compounds annually, not the terminal growth rate in the formula. The practical fair P/B of 2.5x is cross-checked against the quality tier approach (15-18% ROE → 2.5-3.5x P/B).

If the P/B remains at 1.92x, the return equals the BV compounding rate of 12.4%. The stock can tolerate P/B compression from 1.92x down to approximately 1.5x before the 10-year return drops below the 10% hurdle rate.

**Rule of Thumb for Quick Mental Math:**

```
Approximate Annual Return ≈ (ROE × Retention) + Annual P/B Change
where Annual P/B Change = (Exit P/B / Entry P/B)^(1/n) - 1
```

For Mastek at entry P/B 1.92x, ROE 15.5%, Retention 80%:
- If P/B stays constant → Return ≈ 12.4% (BV compounding rate)
- If P/B halves over 10 years → ~6.7% annual drag → Return ≈ 5.7% (rule of thumb); exact from table: 4.9%
- If P/B drops to one-third over 10 years → ~10.9% drag → Return ≈ 1.5%

The key takeaway: at 1.92x P/B, the stock has reasonable valuation safety — even a significant de-rating leaves you with positive (if modest) returns, because the underlying BV compounding at 12.4% absorbs the drag.
