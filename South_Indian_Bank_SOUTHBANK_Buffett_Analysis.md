## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

**Type:** Old-generation Private Sector Bank (Scheduled Commercial Bank)
**Founded:** 1929, Thrissur, Kerala — the first scheduled bank among private banks in Kerala.
**Core Business:** Retail and corporate banking, treasury operations, forex business, para-banking (debit cards, third-party distribution).

**Branch Network:** 948 branches and 1,315 ATMs across India. Majority located in semi-urban areas (460 branches). Heavy concentration in Kerala (53% of customer touchpoints) with South ex-Kerala at 30% and Rest of India at 17%.

**Customer Base:** 75 lakh (7.5 million) customers.

**Loan Book Composition (9MFY24):**
- Corporate Loans: 38%
- Personal Loans: 23% (Housing 29%, Gold 19%, Personal Loan 12%, Mortgage 11%, Vehicle 9%, LAD 8%, Credit Card 8%, Others 4%)
- Business Loans: 20%
- Agriculture: 19%
- 96% A & Above rated Large Corporate Loans

**Deposit Mix (9MFY24):**
- Retail Term: 64%
- Savings: 26%
- Current: ~6%
- Bulk: 4%

**Yield & Cost (Q3FY24):**
- Yield on Funds: 7.53%
- Cost of Funds: 4.71%
- Yield on Advances: 9.28%
- Cost of Deposits: 5.18%

**Would Buffett Understand It?** Absolutely. A bank takes deposits, lends them out at a higher rate, and keeps the spread. It is the oldest and simplest financial business in the world.

**Core Economic Engine:** This is primarily a NIM-driven (spread income) bank with a growing fee income component. The bank earns money by borrowing cheap (deposits) and lending dear (advances). The primary moat question for SIB is whether its deposit franchise — rooted in Kerala — can generate cheap, sticky funding at scale.

Buffett: *"Banking is a very good business if you don't do anything dumb."* The question for South Indian Bank is whether the decade of "dumb" (FY18-FY22 NPA crisis) is truly behind it, and whether the bank has emerged as a genuinely better institution.

**Key Context:** SIB is an old-generation private bank that went through a severe asset quality crisis from ~FY18-FY22, where GNPA spiked above 5% and net profits collapsed to near-zero (₹45 Cr in FY22). The bank has since undergone a dramatic turnaround — GNPA has fallen from 5.48% (Dec 2022) to 2.67% (Dec 2025), and profits have surged from ₹45 Cr (FY22) to ₹1,303 Cr (FY25). This is a classic TURNAROUND story — the critical question is whether the turnaround is durable or temporary.

**Trend Narrative:**

This is a tale of two eras:

**Era 1 — The Crisis (FY18-FY22):** Net profit collapsed from ₹392 Cr (FY17) to just ₹45 Cr (FY22). ROE crashed from 9% to 1%. ROA fell to 0.05%. The bank was essentially earning nothing — all operating income was being consumed by provisions for bad loans. EPS hit a nadir of ₹0.17.

**Era 2 — The Turnaround (FY23-Present):** A dramatic recovery. Net profit surged from ₹45 Cr to ₹1,303 Cr in three years. ROE recovered from 1% to 14%. ROA climbed from 0.05% to 1.08%. EPS went from ₹0.17 to ₹4.98 (FY25) and TTM ₹5.31.

**Critical Question:** Is this earnings recovery driven by genuine operating improvement or simply by lower provisions (which is unsustainable if the credit cycle turns)?

The answer is BOTH — and the corrected NII data is more encouraging than a superficial read suggests. **NII (the core spread engine) has actually GROWN through the turnaround: ₹2,240 Cr (FY22) → ₹3,012 Cr (FY23) → ₹3,332 Cr (FY24) → ₹3,485 Cr (FY25)** — a healthy ~16% CAGR over three years. This is genuine core banking improvement, not just a provision story. GNPA improvement from 5.48% to 2.67% further reduces provision requirements (the second profit lever). Other Income has also grown (₹813 Cr in FY23 to ₹1,813 Cr in FY25) — part of this is recovery income from written-off accounts, which is partly non-recurring. So the profit surge rests on three legs: (1) genuine NII growth, (2) lower provisions as asset quality healed, and (3) elevated Other Income including recoveries. Of these, legs (2) and (3) carry a cyclical tailwind that will fade. Revenue (interest earned) growth has been modest (5-year CAGR ~6%), so this remains fundamentally a recovery/normalisation story rather than a high top-line growth story — but the NII trajectory means the "core engine is weakening" concern is NOT supported by the multi-year data; the core engine has in fact strengthened through FY22-FY25.

> *One honest qualifier on the most recent period: TTM NII (₹3,390 Cr) is ~3% BELOW FY25's ₹3,485 Cr, reflecting softer Jun–Sep 2025 quarterly NII as deposit-cost increases temporarily outpaced asset re-pricing. So while the 3-year trend is firmly up, the core engine has plateaued-to-slightly-softened in the trailing twelve months. The "strengthening" verdict applies to the FY22→FY25 arc; the TTM is better described as "holding near peak with mild sequential pressure." This is consistent with the NIM ceiling imposed by the ~32% CASA ratio (see §10).*

> *Note: NIM (~2.9-3.0% on average assets) has been broadly stable-to-improving since the FY22 trough (~2.3%), consistent with the rising NII. The bank has not expanded NIM dramatically — its ~32% CASA ratio caps spread expansion — but it has arrested the pre-FY22 NIM erosion.*

Buffett: *"When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."* SIB's reputation for bad asset quality (FY18-FY22) is being overcome by improved management — but the turnaround must prove itself through an entire credit cycle before it can be trusted.

**3. Funding Stability: 🟡 MIXED (Concern Area)**

This structural funding disadvantage limits NIM and profitability.

**4. Hidden Risks: 🟡 MODERATE**
- The Co-branded Credit Card issue — RBI restricted the bank from onboarding new co-branded credit card customers until compliance is achieved. This is a regulatory yellow flag.

- Heavy Kerala concentration (53% of touchpoints, 36% of advances) — geographic concentration risk.

Buffett: *"When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."* SIB is actively changing its reputation — GNPA improvement from 5%+ to 2.67% is genuine. But the low CASA ratio (32%) represents a structural weakness in the business economics that will be harder to fix.

**Analysis:**

> *Note on retention ratio: Historical retention (FY23-FY25) has been 92-93%. ### ROA-to-ROE Bridge (The Quality Check)

**FY25:** ROE 14% = ROA 1.08% × Leverage ≈ 14%.

> **Closing the bridge precisely:** Using year-end figures, leverage is 12.3x (₹124,655 Cr assets / ₹10,108 Cr equity) and 1.08% × 12.3 = 13.3% — about 0.7pp short of the reported 14%. The gap closes once both ratios are put on a consistent AVERAGE basis: reported ROE and ROA are calculated on *average* equity/assets, not year-end. Average assets ÷ average equity = ₹121,034 Cr / ₹9,467.5 Cr = **~12.8x**, and 1.08% × 12.8 = **~13.8% ≈ reported 14%**. So the decomposition is internally sound; the apparent 0.7pp gap was simply a year-end-vs-average mismatch. (When discussing balance-sheet structure rather than the DuPont bridge, the year-end leverage of 12.3x is the right figure to cite.)

**This is QUALITY ROE.** The ROE of 14% is driven by an ROA of 1.08% with MODERATE leverage of ~12.3-12.8x. Compare this to:
- A bank with 14% ROE from 0.7% ROA × 20x leverage = DANGEROUS
- SIB with 14% ROE from 1.08% ROA × 12.3x leverage = QUALITY

The leverage has actually DECREASED while ROE has increased — which means the ROE improvement is coming from a better business (higher ROA), not from balance-sheet risk (higher leverage). That is the *right kind* of ROE composition. The caveat (per the tier discussion below) is that the 1.08% ROA itself is cyclically elevated by low provisioning — so this is high-quality ROE *composition* sitting on top of a not-yet-proven-through-cycle ROA level.

### Quality Tier Assessment

| Quality Tier | ROA | ROE | Deserved P/B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elite | >2.0% | >18% | 3.0-4.0x |
| Excellent | 1.5-2.0% | 15-18% | 2.5-3.5x |
| **Good** | **1.0-1.5%** | **12-15%** | **1.5-2.5x** |
| Mediocre | 0.5-1.0% | 8-12% | 0.8-1.5x |
| Poor | <0.5% | <8% | <1.0x |

**On CURRENT (peak-cycle) metrics, SIB sits in the "Good" tier** (ROA 1.08%, ROE 14%, deserved P/B 1.5-2.5x on a textbook basis). But this is the wrong anchor for a Buffett-style assessment.

**The Buffett-correct anchor is THROUGH-CYCLE performance — and on that basis SIB is "Mediocre."** The 5-year average ROA is only 0.58%, squarely in the Mediocre tier (deserved P/B 0.8-1.5x). Buffett classifies business quality on demonstrated, sustained performance — not on the peak of a recovery cycle. The current 1.08% ROA has been achieved for essentially one to two years, on the back of a benign credit environment and NPA recoveries; it has NOT been sustained through a downturn under the current management.

The honest conclusion: **SIB is a Mediocre-tier bank showing Good-tier current metrics.** If the 1.08% ROA proves durable through the next cycle, it graduates to the Good tier and the deserved P/B rises toward 1.5x. If ROA reverts to 0.7-0.8% (the likely outcome if credit costs normalise to 1.0%), ROE drops to ~9-10% and the deserved P/B is 0.8-1.5x — meaning today's 0.90x is roughly fair value, NOT a significant discount. **The reader should anchor to the Mediocre-tier deserved P/B of 0.8-1.5x and treat any re-rating above that as upside optionality contingent on the turnaround proving durable.**

Buffett: *"A great bank earns high ROE primarily through high ROA (not excessive leverage), maintains this through credit cycles, and can reinvest retained earnings at the same high ROE to compound book value."* SIB earns its current ROE through improving ROA with declining leverage — the right formula directionally — but the phrase "maintains this through credit cycles" is precisely what remains unproven. Until SIB demonstrates one full cycle at >1% ROA, it is a Mediocre-tier bank on probation, not a Good-tier bank.

### Fair P/B Calculation (Gordon Growth Model)

```
Fair P/B = (ROE - g) / (CoE - g)
Where:
  ROE = 12% (conservative sustainable — below current 14% to account for credit cycle normalisation)
  g   = ROE × Retention = 12% × 90% = 10.8%  (90% retention, consistent with Sections 15, 17, and 24)
  CoE = 12% (base assumption)

Fair P/B = (0.12 - 0.108) / (0.12 - 0.108) = 0.012 / 0.012 = 1.0x
```

At 12% sustainable ROE and 12% CoE, the Fair P/B is approximately **1.0x Book Value.**

> **Cost of Equity Sensitivity — A Critical Caveat (the Gordon model is hypersensitive to CoE when ROE ≈ CoE):**
> The 12% CoE above is appropriate for India's large, well-managed private banks (HDFC Bank, Kotak). SIB's risk profile — small-cap (~₹10,000-12,000 Cr market cap), 0% promoter, regional concentration, and a recent 6% GNPA history — arguably warrants a HIGHER CoE. Building it up: risk-free ~6.8% + equity risk premium ~5.5% + size premium ~1.5% + company-specific risk ~1.5% ≈ **15%**. The Gordon Fair P/B is extremely sensitive to this input (table below uses g = 10.8%, i.e., 12% ROE × 90% retention, consistent with the rest of the document):
>
> | CoE Assumption | Sustainable ROE | g (= ROE × 90%) | Fair P/B |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 12% (large-bank rate) | 12% | 10.8% | ~1.00x |
> | 13% | 12% | 10.8% | ~0.55x |
> | 14% | 12% | 10.8% | ~0.38x |
> | 15% (SIB-appropriate) | 12% | 10.8% | ~0.29x |
>
> **This is the single biggest swing factor in the bull-vs-bear debate.** At a defensible 15% CoE, the Gordon Fair P/B is only ~0.29x — implying the stock at 0.90x is EXPENSIVE on this model. The Gordon model is mathematically unstable when ROE ≈ CoE (denominator approaches zero), so it should NOT be the primary valuation anchor for SIB. This is precisely why the book-value-compounding model (Section 15, Method 1) and the asset-quality/discount-to-book angle are the more reliable bases for this investment — they do not depend on a knife-edge CoE assumption.

If ROE sustains at 14% (with 12% CoE):
```
g = 14% × 92% = 12.9%
Fair P/B = (0.14 - 0.129) / (0.12 - 0.129)
```
Note: g (12.9%) > CoE (12%), so the Gordon model breaks down (negative denominator) — it cannot produce a finite fair value. This is a known limitation when ROE exceeds CoE with high retention; it does NOT mean fair value is "very high." It means the model is inapplicable in this regime and we must fall back to the book-value compounding model and rules of thumb.

### Buffett's Bank Valuation Rules of Thumb

|---|---|---|
| ROE ≈ 11% (= CoE) | ~1.0x Book | Slight discount — reasonable |
| ROE = 13-14% | 1.5-2.0x Book | Discount — but see caveat below |
| ROE = 15-16% | 2.0-3.0x Book | — |

> **Reconciling the two methods (they disagree, and the disagreement matters):** The textbook rules-of-thumb table above (derived for HIGH-QUALITY, durable-moat banks at a ~10-11% CoE) suggests 1.5-2.0x for a 13-14% ROE bank. But SIB's own Gordon model — using a SIB-appropriate CoE of 13-15% — supports only ~0.5-1.0x. **These ranges do NOT overlap, and the honest answer is that SIB's fair P/B sits between them: approximately 1.0-1.3x, NOT the 1.5-2.5x implied by the textbook table.** The textbook multiples assume franchise quality (high CASA, proven through-cycle underwriting) that SIB does not possess. The reader should anchor to the lower, CoE-adjusted fair P/B of ~1.0-1.3x.

**At 0.90x P/B with a 14% current (but cyclically-flattered) ROE, SIB trades at a modest discount to a CoE-adjusted fair P/B of ~1.0-1.3x — NOT the 1.5-2.5x a textbook quality-tier table would imply.** If the 14% ROE is sustainable, the stock is moderately cheap (upside to ~1.2-1.3x). If ROE reverts to 9-10% as the provision tailwind fades, then 0.90x is roughly fair value. The bull case is "modestly undervalued," not "deeply discounted."

This is the central tension of the investment thesis: **Are you buying a sustainable-14% ROE bank at 0.9x book (moderately cheap), or a reverting-to-9-10% ROE bank at 0.9x book (fair value)?** The truth is probably in between — which is why this is a reasonable small position, not a table-pounding bargain.
