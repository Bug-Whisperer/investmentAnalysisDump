## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

JK Tyre & Industries Ltd, the flagship of the JK Organisation, is India's 4th-largest tyre manufacturer by revenue and the 19th-largest globally. Established in 1951 and headquartered in New Delhi, the company develops, manufactures, markets, and distributes automotive tyres, tubes, flaps, and retreads across India, Mexico, and 100+ export markets.

**Business Model:** JK Tyre operates a classic capital-intensive manufacturing business. It purchases natural rubber (~42% of raw-material cost) and crude-oil derivatives (~30%), transforms them through energy-intensive vulcanisation, and sells finished tyres through two channels:
- **OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturer):** Supplies tyres directly to vehicle makers for fitment on new vehicles. Lower margin, but provides brand visibility and guaranteed volumes.
- **Replacement Market:** Sells through 6,000+ dealers and 900+ brand shops (Steel Wheels, Truck Wheels, Xpress Wheels). Higher margin, more pricing power, and the majority of revenue.

**Product Portfolio:** Truck/Bus Radial (TBR — India market leader), Passenger Car Radial (PCR), Light Commercial Vehicle (LCV), Farm, Off-The-Road (OTR), Two/Three-Wheeler, and Racing tyres.

**Geographic Footprint:** 9 manufacturing plants in India + 3 plants in Mexico (subsidiary JK Tornel). Exports to 100+ countries.

**Buffett Classification:** This is fundamentally a **commodity-adjacent manufacturing business** with some brand differentiation. It is NOT a toll bridge (no pricing power over rubber costs), NOT a consumer franchise (tyres are not aspirational), and NOT a switching-cost business (customers switch brands easily). However, there IS some brand loyalty in the commercial-vehicle segment where fleet operators value reliability and after-sales service. The replacement market provides recurring revenue — the closest this business comes to a Buffett franchise.

Buffett: *"I try to invest in businesses that are so wonderful that an idiot can run them, because sooner or later, one will."* Tyre manufacturing is NOT that kind of business — it requires skilled capital allocation, careful raw-material hedging, and tight cost control. The margin for error is thin in a commodity-dependent, capital-intensive industry. This places JK Tyre **at the edge of, or just outside, the Buffett circle of competence** for a *high-quality* compounder — though it is perfectly understandable as a *cyclical value* proposition.

### Trend Narrative

**Revenue:** Sales CAGR of 9.0% over 10 years (FY16-FY26) and 12.4% over 5 years (FY21→FY26). Revenue more than doubled from ₹6,898 Cr (FY16) to a **record ₹16,327 Cr (FY26)**, driven by volume growth, price hikes (partially passing through raw-material inflation), and the FY25 consolidation of Cavendish Industries. FY26 was, in management's words, a "landmark year" with record volumes across segments. Still, through-cycle top-line growth is moderate — roughly in line with nominal GDP.

**Margins:** OPM (pre-D&A) has swung between **8.9%** (first reached in FY18, again in FY23) and **16.2%** (FY16) — a 7+ pp range. **EBIT Margin** (after D&A — the true Buffett-relevant measure) ranges from **5.3%** (FY18) to **13.1%** (FY16); FY26 sits at **9.5%**, a mid-cycle level (recovered from the FY25 rubber-cost squeeze but below the FY16/FY24 peaks). The ~3 pp gap between OPM and EBIT Margin is the depreciation charge — a real cost EBITDA-based analysis masks. Munger: *"Every time you see the word EBITDA, you should substitute the phrase 'bullshit earnings.'"* This is the hallmark of a commodity-dependent business where margins are dictated by the rubber-crude cycle, not pricing power.

**Net Profit:** NP CAGR of 5.2% over 10 years; the stronger 5-year CAGR of 18.6% (FY21→FY26) reflects the post-downturn recovery (off the FY20 trough of ₹141 Cr) plus the FY24/FY26 margin upcycle. FY26 NP of ₹776 Cr (₹823 Cr ex-exceptional) is the 2nd-highest ever (FY24's ₹806 Cr was the peak). The FY18 collapse (NP ₹63 Cr, EPS ₹2.91) illustrates the downside in bad years.

**EPS:** 10-year EPS CAGR of 2.7% — well below revenue growth because **recurring dilution** has eroded per-share gains. Shares grew from 20.5 Cr (FY14) to ~28.9 Cr (FY26) — a ~41% increase via the FY19 preferential allotment, the FY24 QIP, and a further FY26 issuance (equity capital ₹55→58 Cr). Buffett views persistent dilution negatively.

Buffett: *"The investor of today does not profit from yesterday's growth."* The key question: is EPS growth durably improving, or are we looking at cyclical highs that revert? **Margins expanded into FY26 but remain mid-cycle; earnings growth has lagged revenue growth due to dilution and interest costs.**

### Financial Fortress Assessment

**This is improving but is still NOT a fortress balance sheet — though the gap has narrowed dramatically.** Key points:

1. **Leverage — the standout improvement:** Debt/Equity peaked at **3.0x** (FY18) after the Mexico acquisition and expansion capex. It has fallen for six straight years to **0.81x in FY26** — a genuine, disciplined deleveraging. This is the single biggest positive in the entire analysis. It remains above Buffett's preferred <0.5x, but the trajectory is unambiguous.

2. **Interest Burden:** Interest consumed ₹428 Cr in FY26 against EBIT of ₹1,559 Cr — EBIT-based interest coverage of **3.6x** (up from 2.4x in FY25). Buffett prefers >5x. The EBITDA-based coverage (5.3x) looks healthier but ignores the ₹472 Cr of depreciation that represents real capital consumption. Coverage is now adequate, not yet comfortable.

3. **BVPS Growth:** Book value has compounded from ₹53.5 (FY14) to **₹210 (FY26)** — a 12.1% CAGR. This is solid, but partly driven by equity raises (QIP, preferential allotment, FY26 issuance) rather than purely organic retained earnings.

4. **Net Debt:** Net debt (₹4,761 Cr FY26) is down from the ₹5,683 Cr FY18 peak. At ~6.5× Owner Earnings, it is still a meaningful claim ahead of equity holders, but manageable given improved coverage.

Buffett: *"Only when the tide goes out do you discover who's been swimming naked."* JK Tyre was swimming nearly naked in FY18 (D/E 3.0x, EBIT interest coverage 0.9x — EBIT did not even cover interest). The tide has come back in convincingly with the deleveraging to 0.81x, but at >0.5x D/E and 3.6x coverage the balance sheet is **investment-grade-improving, not yet fortress-grade.**

### Cash Flow Quality Assessment

FCF has been **highly volatile**, ranging from **−₹701 Cr** (FY17, Mexico acquisition/expansion) to **+₹1,464 Cr** (FY21, pandemic-led demand recovery + deferred capex). This is characteristic of a capital-intensive business — heavy-capex years produce negative FCF, followed by harvest years of strong FCF when capex normalises.

**Stock-Based Compensation & Buybacks:** JK Tyre has **negligible SBC** (a traditional promoter-run Indian company) and **no buyback programme**. GAAP earnings ≈ economic earnings on the dilution dimension; the share-count growth (Section 2) comes from equity *raises* (QIP, preferential allotment, FY26 issuance), not option-vesting. Because SBC is immaterial (<5% of NI), **Base Owner Earnings — not Dilution-Adjusted OE — is the correct DCF input** (see Sections 8 and 15).

**CFO-to-Operating-Profit conversion** is generally healthy (Screener CFO/OP: 79-129% in most years), confirming reported earnings are backed by real cash. Exceptions are capex/working-capital-heavy years (FY17, FY22).

Buffett: *"Owner earnings represent the relevant item for valuation purposes — not GAAP earnings, not operating earnings, not operating cash flow."* JK Tyre's Owner Earnings sit **slightly below reported Net Profit every year** under our maintenance-capex assumption (OE = NI − 0.1×D&A; see Section 8) — appropriate for a capital-intensive business where replacing worn-out plant costs more than book depreciation. FY26 OE of ₹729 Cr (vs NP ₹776 Cr) is the DCF starting point.

### Maintenance CapEx Methodology — Explicitly Justified for THIS Business

**Owner Earnings = Net Income + D&A − Maintenance CapEx**, with **Maintenance CapEx set at 1.1× Depreciation**. This is justified specifically for JK Tyre:

- **Why tie it to depreciation, not a % of total CapEx?** JK Tyre's *reported* gross CapEx swings violently with discrete expansion projects — ₹2,558 Cr in FY17 (Mexico consolidation), ₹1,585 Cr in FY26 (the ₹4,980 Cr expansion ramp), versus ₹262-430 Cr in quiet years. A fixed "% of total CapEx" rule would misclassify lumpy *growth* spending as *maintenance* in expansion years (badly understating Owner Earnings) and the reverse in quiet years. Depreciation — the accounting measure of how fast existing plant is consumed — is the stable, business-appropriate proxy for the spend needed to *sustain current capacity*.

- **Why the 1.1× (10%) uplift?** Buffett's definition deducts the capex required to *fully maintain competitive position and unit volume*. In an inflationary economy, replacing a worn-out tyre press or mixing line costs **more** than its historical-cost depreciation. A 10% uplift is a deliberately modest inflation adjustment for a business with long-lived plant; it keeps Owner Earnings slightly **below** reported Net Income every year (OE = NI − 0.1×D&A), which is the economically correct direction for a capital-intensive manufacturer.

- **Sensitivity (disclosed):** A *higher* maintenance assumption reduces Owner Earnings. At 1.0× D&A, FY26 OE = ₹776 Cr (= NI); at 1.1×, ₹729 Cr; at 1.3× D&A, ₹634 Cr. We use 1.1× as a reasonable central estimate; the DCF (Section 15) is stress-tested via conservative growth.

**FY26 Owner Earnings = ₹776 + ₹472 − ₹519 = ₹729 Cr** (₹25.27/share). *(Normalised for the ₹46.67 Cr exceptional loss, FY26 OE ≈ ₹776 Cr.)*

**SBC:** JK Tyre has **negligible** stock-based compensation (traditional promoter-run company) and no buybacks. GAAP earnings ≈ economic earnings on the dilution dimension; **no SBC adjustment is required, and Base Owner Earnings (not Dilution-Adjusted OE) is the correct DCF input** — the SBC materiality gate (<5% of NI) is comfortably cleared.

Buffett: *"Owner earnings represent (a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation… less (c) the average annual amount of capitalised expenditures for plant and equipment that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and unit volume."* For JK Tyre, item (c) is substantial and growing — the rising depreciation line and the ₹4,980 Cr expansion both confirm that staying competitive in tyres requires continuous heavy reinvestment.

> **CapEx vs CFI note (methodology):** The gross CapEx row is derived from the balance sheet as Δ(Fixed Assets + CWIP) + Depreciation, whereas Section 4's Free Cash Flow uses reported **Investing Cash Flow (CFI)**. The two diverge in several years (typically ₹100-230 Cr; e.g. FY17 balance-sheet CapEx ₹2,558 Cr vs CFI ₹807 Cr; FY24 ₹971 Cr vs ₹1,203 Cr) because CFI nets asset-sale proceeds, capital-advance/creditor movements, and non-fixed-asset investing flows against gross spending. Owner Earnings (Section 8) therefore uses the gross-asset-based maintenance proxy (1.1× Depreciation) while FCF (Section 4) uses CFI as reported — the two are intentionally on different bases and are **not** expected to tie out year by year (do not compute FCF = CFO − this CapEx row).

## 10. COMPETITIVE POSITION & MOAT ANALYSIS

### Industry Structure
The Indian tyre industry is a **moderately concentrated oligopoly** — 5 players control ~65-70% of the market: MRF (the clear leader, ~30%+ on industry estimates), Apollo Tyres, CEAT, JK Tyre, and Balkrishna (niche OHT). *(Market-share figures are industry estimates, not company-disclosed.)*

### JK Tyre's Competitive Position
- **#1 in Truck/Bus Radial (TBR)** — the most profitable, highest-replacement-demand segment
- **#4 overall** in India by revenue (behind MRF, Apollo, and roughly level with CEAT)
- **19th globally** by sales — up several positions over the decade
- **Mexico operations** (JK Tornel) add geographic diversification (and complexity)

### Moat Assessment
| Moat Factor | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Brand** | Moderate in CV/TBR; weaker in PCR where MRF/Apollo dominate OEM |
| **Switching Costs** | Low — tyres are commodity-like; fleets switch on price/availability |
| **Cost Advantage** | None structural — raw-material costs are similar industry-wide |
| **Network Effects** | None |
| **Distribution** | Moderate-to-strong — 6,000+ dealers, 900+ brand shops |
| **Scale** | Moderate — #4 gives procurement/distribution scale, but below MRF/Apollo |

**Moat Verdict: NARROW moat, primarily from TBR brand leadership and distribution reach.** It is durable enough to hold share and grow with the market, but not wide enough to deliver franchise-like through-cycle returns. It is under constant pressure from deeper-pocketed peers (MRF, Apollo) and from Chinese imports in the value segment.

**Is the moat enduring or eroding? — Roughly STABLE, neither widening nor materially eroding.** Market share has been broadly held; the TBR leadership and dealer network are sticky, but no evidence of the moat *widening* into pricing power.

**Susceptibility to Change (the "absence of change" test):** Buffett: *"Our criterion of 'enduring' causes us to rule out companies in industries prone to rapid and continuous change. A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all."* The tyre industry faces *moderate* change risk: (a) EV transition needs different tyre formulations (manageable, long runway), (b) Chinese import competition (mitigated by anti-dumping duties), (c) retreading/circular-economy shifts. But the fundamental need for tyres on vehicles is unlikely to change for decades — the business will look broadly similar in 10-20 years, which is a Buffett positive. **Honest assessment: the moat is real but narrow; this is a survivor and steady grower, not a fortress franchise.**

## 12. MANAGEMENT QUALITY & BUFFETT'S $1 TEST

**Leadership:** Dr. Raghupati Singhania (Chairman & MD) and Anshuman Singhania (MD) — promoter family, long-tenured. The JK Organisation is a well-regarded Indian business house with a multi-decade operating history in tyres.

### Buffett's $1 Test

- **Period:** FY14 (base) to FY26 — retained earnings counted FY15-FY26 (12 years of accumulation)
- **Cumulative Retained Earnings (FY15-FY26, organic, ex-equity-raises):** ₹3,779 Cr
- **Market-Cap Change:** ₹9,045 Cr (from ~₹2,091 Cr at FY14 — ₹102 × ~20.5 Cr shares — to ₹11,136 Cr now)
- **$1 Test Ratio:** **₹2.39 of market value created per ₹1 retained**

**Verdict: PASSES the $1 test (ratio > 1.0).** Caveats for honesty: (i) a meaningful slice of the market-cap gain reflects **PE re-rating** off a deeply-depressed FY14 multiple (~8x), not pure business-value creation; (ii) **equity raises** (QIP, preferential allotment, FY26 issuance) added to the market cap independent of retained earnings — the test uses *organic* retained earnings in the denominator, so this inflates the numerator. On balance the test passes, but it is flattered by re-rating.

**PE-normalised cross-check (how much is real value creation?).** Had the depressed FY14 entry multiple (~8× P/E) simply held, FY26 market cap would be ~₹6,208 Cr (8 × ₹776 Cr NP). On that basis only **~₹4,117 Cr of the ₹9,045 Cr market-cap gain is *business-value creation***; the remaining **~₹4,928 Cr (~54%) is PE re-rating** off the low FY14 base. The **PE-normalised $1 ratio is therefore ~1.1×** (₹4,117 Cr ÷ ₹3,779 Cr retained), not the 2.39× headline — i.e. capital allocation *passes, but unremarkably*. The honest read: management compounded book value and grew the business, but roughly half of the shareholder return since FY14 came from the multiple expanding, not from capital deployed at high incremental returns.

### Capital Allocation Track Record
