## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

BLS International Services Ltd is the world's largest listed visa application outsourcing company. Founded in 1983 (originally Welken Traders), the company has evolved into a trusted tech-enabled services partner for governments and diplomatic missions worldwide. The business model is elegantly simple — governments outsource the administrative processing of visa, passport, and consular applications to BLS, which operates Visa Application Centres (VACs) on their behalf, collecting fees from applicants.

**Business Segments:**

1. **Visa & Consular Services (~78% of FY25 revenue, declining to ~61% in 9MFY26 as digital grows):** The core franchise. BLS operates VACs for 46+ client governments across 66+ countries with 50,000+ application centres globally. Services include visa processing, passport services, biometric collection, attestation/apostille, and value-added services (courier, document verification, premium lounge). The company acquired Turkey-based iDATA in July 2024 for ~₹720 Cr, adding 37+ centres in 15+ European countries with ~55-60% pre-D&A operating margins (the press headlines refer to this as "55-60% EBITDA margins" — same number, this framework prefers EBIT after capital charge).

2. **Digital Services (~22% of FY25 revenue, rising to ~39% in 9MFY26):** Operated through subsidiary BLS E-Services Ltd (listed Feb 2024). This segment provides e-governance services, Business Correspondent (BC) services for banks, Aadhaar enrolment, and citizen services across India through physical touchpoints in rural and semi-urban areas. This segment is growing rapidly (109% YoY in Q3FY26), driven by the Aadifidelis Solutions consolidation.

**Would Buffett Understand It?** Absolutely. This is a classic **toll bridge business** — every person who wants a visa to travel must pass through BLS's processing centres and pay a fee. The company doesn't take travel risk, credit risk, or inventory risk. It collects fees upfront, operates on an asset-light *operating* model with leased centres, and benefits from the secular growth in global mobility. *(Note: Post-iDATA, the balance sheet shows Fixed Assets of ₹1,363 Cr (~62% of revenue, up from ~18% pre-acquisition) **plus a separate ₹720 Cr Goodwill line** from the iDATA acquisition — total long-term asset burden ₹2,083 Cr on a ₹2,804 Cr total asset base. As Section 3 clearly separates these two line-items, the ₹1,363 Cr Fixed Assets figure represents tangible plus acquired intangibles excluding goodwill; the ₹720 Cr goodwill is shown distinctly. The underlying operations remain asset-light; the balance-sheet expansion reflects acquired intangibles and goodwill, not capital-intensive infrastructure.)* Visa charges are collected before services are rendered, creating negative working capital — a hallmark of businesses Buffett loves.

> *"The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow, but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage."* — Warren Buffett

BLS is a **government-contracted toll collector** on global human mobility. The contracts are typically multi-year (3-5 years) with high renewal rates, creating revenue visibility. The switching costs for governments are enormous — changing visa processors involves security clearances, technology integration, staff training, and diplomatic considerations.

**Trend Narrative:** This is an extraordinary margin expansion story. OPM (pre-D&A) has expanded from 6.7% in FY15 to 29.6% in FY25 — a 4.4x improvement. More importantly, EBIT Margin (the true after-capital-charge operating profitability) has expanded from 5.6% to 26.1%, confirming that the margin expansion is genuine and not being consumed by rising depreciation. The business was severely impacted by COVID in FY20-FY21 (travel ban = no visa applications), but the recovery has been spectacular. Post-COVID, the business has not merely recovered — it has structurally re-rated to much higher margins, driven by the iDATA acquisition (55-60% margins), increased value-added services, and operating leverage from a larger application base. Net profit growth has consistently outpaced revenue growth in recent years, confirming genuine margin expansion rather than just top-line momentum.

> *"References to EBITDA make us shudder — does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?"* — Warren Buffett (2000 Letter). This is why we track EBIT separately from Screener's Operating Profit (which is pre-D&A, i.e. EBITDA-equivalent). For BLS, the gap between OPM (29.6%) and EBIT Margin (26.1%) is only 3.5pp, reflecting the asset-light nature of the business — depreciation is a small fraction of operating profit. For capital-heavy businesses, this gap can be 10-15pp, which EBITDA-based analysis dangerously hides.

The only red flag is the low effective tax rate (8-13% in recent years vs. the statutory 25%), which benefits from overseas subsidiaries in low-tax jurisdictions. This is a legitimate structural advantage, not a one-time item.

*‡‡ **Apparent contradiction explained — WC Days (−42) vs Current Ratio (1.6):** These two metrics are NOT contradictory. Screener's Working Capital Days excludes cash and short-term investments from the numerator — it measures only operating working capital (Receivables + Inventory − Payables), which can be negative when customers pay upfront. The Current Ratio includes ALL current assets (most importantly the ₹277 Cr cash balance) divided by current liabilities. So a company can simultaneously have negative operating WC (good — customers fund operations) AND a Current Ratio above 1.0 (good — total liquidity covers short-term obligations). For BLS, both signals are positive: customers prepay (negative WC days), and the cash buffer provides ample liquidity (Current Ratio 1.6x).*

## 10. COMPETITIVE POSITION & MOAT ANALYSIS

### Moat Type: **Government Contract Toll Bridge + Switching Costs + Regulatory Barrier**

**Moat Sources:**

1. **Government Contracts as Toll Bridge:** Every visa applicant for BLS's client governments MUST process through a BLS centre. There is no alternative channel. This is not a consumer choice business — it is a government-mandated processing monopoly for the specific contract geography. BLS currently serves 46+ governments across 66+ countries.

2. **Enormous Switching Costs:** Governments are extremely reluctant to switch visa processors. The reasons are: security clearances and background checks take 12-18 months; technology integration with each government's internal systems is deeply embedded; staff training on each country's specific requirements is substantial; and the reputational risk of a botched transition (imagine a security breach during a visa processor changeover) makes bureaucrats profoundly risk-averse. Contract renewal rates are very high.

3. **Regulatory & Security Barriers:** Handling sovereign citizen data (biometrics, passport information, travel history) requires the highest security clearances. New entrants face a multi-year, multi-million dollar investment just to qualify for their first contract. This is a quasi-regulated monopoly.

4. **Scale Advantage:** BLS's 50,000+ centres and 66-country presence give it a cost and capability advantage when bidding for new contracts. Governments prefer operators with proven global track records.

5. **Network Effects (Weak but Present):** Each new government contract makes BLS more attractive to the next government — credibility compounds.

### Key Competitors

| Competitor | Geography | Threat Level | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|
| VFS Global (Kuoni/EQT) | Global | Manageable — #1 player, but market is growing | Market leader; privately held; BLS is #2 |
| TLScontact (Teleperformance) | Europe/Africa | Manageable | Strong in French-speaking markets |
| CGI Group | Select markets | Low | Smaller player |

The visa outsourcing market is effectively a **duopoly** between VFS Global and BLS International, with TLScontact as a distant third. The market itself is growing at **~17% CAGR** (from approximately $3.7B in 2023 to $8.3B by 2028, per industry research aggregates; the implied CAGR from a 2023 base over 5 years is 17.5%) and the share of outsourced processing has grown from ~22% (15 years ago) to ~35% currently, with industry forecasts of ~50% over the next decade. This means both players can grow without taking share from each other — the pie is expanding.

### Is the Business Susceptible to Change?

**Mostly no, but with one credible disruption pathway that must be acknowledged honestly: e-visa proliferation.**

The fundamental business model — collect fees to process government paperwork for cross-border travel — is timeless. People will still need visas to travel; governments will still want to outsource the administrative burden. Biometric technology may evolve, but BLS will adopt it (they already offer facial recognition, AI-based processing, etc.).

**However, the rise of e-visa systems is a real threat that the analysis must confront, not dismiss.** Multiple countries (India e-Visa, Turkey e-Visa, Singapore eVisa, US ESTA, EU ETIAS-coming) have introduced electronic visa systems that bypass physical Visa Application Centres entirely for certain visa classes. **Important clarification on the visa segment trajectory:** The Visa & Consular segment's share of total revenue has declined from **78% of FY25 revenue to ~61% in 9MFY26** — a 17pp mix shift in just three quarters. **However, this mix shift is NOT direct evidence of e-visa cannibalisation.** A careful decomposition shows: visa segment absolute revenue actually grew **+5.1% YoY** (FY25 ₹1,700 Cr → 9MFY26 annualised ₹1,787 Cr); the 17pp mix shift is fully explained by Digital Services hypergrowth (+71% YoY, driven by BLS E-Services + Aadifidelis consolidation), which is diluting the visa share of the mix rather than visa shrinking absolutely. **The honest read:** visa volumes appear stable, with the e-visa risk being a *forward-looking* threat not yet manifesting in BLS's current numbers. The earlier characterisation of the mix shift as "direct evidence of e-visa pressure" was a logical error — the evidence shows the opposite (visa segment growing in absolute terms). The e-visa risk remains real and should not be dismissed (Section 19 ⚠️ qualification preserved), but its *current* manifestation in BLS's reported data is absent.

**Why this is still manageable for BLS:**
- E-visas primarily affect short-tourist visa categories — business visas, work permits, long-term visas, and high-fee consular services (which carry biometric and document verification requirements) still require physical processing. These are also the higher-margin segments.
- BLS itself is moving up the value chain into VAS, premium services, and digital channels — capturing a share of the e-visa back-end processing.
- The aggregate visa market is still growing at ~17% CAGR even with e-visa cannibalisation, because global travel volumes are growing faster.

**Why this matters for the thesis:** The "absence of change" Buffett quote applies to the business MODEL, not to every product category. BLS's pivot toward Digital Services and VAS is a rational response to e-visa cannibalisation — but investors should monitor the visa segment's organic growth rate (ex-iDATA) closely. If it slows meaningfully below industry growth, the moat may be narrowing in ways that DCF base-case assumptions do not capture.

The other disruption risk is if governments bring processing back in-house, but the trend is overwhelmingly in the opposite direction (outsourcing share has grown from ~22% 15 years ago to ~35% today, with industry forecasts of ~50%). Budget constraints and efficiency demands make in-sourcing increasingly unlikely.

> *"We do not get enthused about change... we're looking for the absence of change to protect ways that are already making a lot of money."* — Warren Buffett. BLS's visa processing model is about as change-resistant as it gets.

> **Why this matters:** Step 3 uses EPS because the market prices on PE — it tells you what the *market* needs to believe.
