## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

### Business Description

Swaraj Engines Ltd was incorporated in 1985 and began operations in 1989. It was jointly promoted by Punjab Tractors Ltd and Kirloskar Oil Engines Ltd. The company has a singular, laser-focused business: **manufacturing diesel engines specifically for Swaraj tractors** in the range of 22 HP to above 65 HP, along with hi-tech engine components.

In 2007, Mahindra & Mahindra (M&M) acquired a majority stake in Punjab Tractors Ltd, bringing Swaraj Engines into the Mahindra ecosystem. Today, M&M (through its Farm Division) is effectively the sole customer — Swaraj Engines is a **captive engine supplier** to the Swaraj tractor brand, which is one of India's top tractor brands.

### Buffett Classification: Captive Supplier (Toll Bridge Analogy)

This business is best understood as a **captive supplier within the Mahindra ecosystem** — analogous to a toll bridge, except that the toll rate is negotiated with the bridge's primary user (M&M) rather than set by the bridge owner. Every Swaraj tractor that rolls off the assembly line needs a Swaraj engine. The company doesn't need to find customers, build brands, or compete for market share — it simply manufactures engines to order for its parent group's tractor division. It is the equivalent of a captive toll collector on a single, well-trafficked bridge.

> **Buffett: "I try to buy stock in businesses that are so wonderful that an idiot can run them. Because sooner or later, one will."**

Swaraj Engines fits this description almost perfectly. The business is so simple that it barely needs explaining: make engines, ship them to the parent company, collect payment. There is no complex go-to-market strategy, no customer acquisition cost, no marketing budget to speak of, no channel conflicts. The demand is driven entirely by Swaraj tractor sales, which in turn are driven by the Indian agricultural cycle.

> **Buffett: "Never invest in a business you cannot understand."**

A 10-year-old could understand this business: a factory makes tractor engines and sells them to one customer. That's it. This is firmly within any investor's circle of competence.

### Key Business Characteristics
- **Single customer dependency** — Mahindra's Swaraj Division (both a strength and a risk)
- **Zero debt** — The company has essentially no borrowings
- **Asset-light** — Minimal fixed assets relative to revenue
- **High return on capital** — ROCE generally above 35% (range: 32–56%; only dipped to 32% in the FY2016 trough)
- **High dividend payout** — Historically 65–91% payout ratio
- **Negative working capital (historical)** — Cash conversion cycle was deeply negative (FY2014-FY2020) but has moved to roughly neutral in FY2021-FY2025. The earlier advantage has largely diminished due to a structural increase in debtor days.
- **No pricing power concerns** — Transfer pricing within the Mahindra group, but the relationship has consistently delivered healthy margins

### Trend Narrative

**Revenue:** Swaraj Engines has grown revenue from ₹540 Cr in FY2015 to ₹1,682 Cr in FY2025 — a **10-year CAGR of ~12%** (per Screener; FY2014→FY2025 over 11 years works out to ~9.7%). The growth has been lumpy, closely tied to the agricultural/tractor cycle. FY2016 was the trough (₹526 Cr), while FY2025 is the peak at ₹1,682 Cr. The 5-year CAGR is a stronger 17%, reflecting the post-COVID recovery and strong tractor demand.

**Margins:** Operating margins have been remarkably stable in a **13-16% band** over the entire period. There is no clear margin expansion trend — this is a captive supplier with stable, negotiated margins. EBIT margins (the Buffett-preferred operating profitability metric, after accounting for depreciation) have been stable in the **10-14% band**, with no clear trend of expansion or compression. The stability reflects the negotiated transfer-pricing nature of the captive-supplier relationship.

**Earnings Growth:** Net profit has grown from ₹52 Cr (FY2015) to ₹166 Cr (FY2025) — a 10-year CAGR of ~12.3%. The 5-year CAGR is stronger at 19%. Earnings growth has broadly tracked revenue growth, confirming that this is a volume-driven business rather than a margin-expansion story.

**Zero Dilution:** Shares outstanding have remained flat at 1.215 Cr throughout the entire period. This is a massive positive — all per-share metrics have grown in lockstep with absolute profits. There has been ZERO dilution.

**Dividend Policy:** The company pays out 65-91% of profits as dividends. This is characteristic of a capital-light, cash-generative business with limited reinvestment needs — textbook Buffett quality.

> **Buffett: "The best business to own is one that over an extended period can employ large amounts of incremental capital at very high rates of return."**

However, Swaraj Engines is a slightly different animal — it doesn't NEED to employ large amounts of incremental capital. It generates cash, pays most of it out, and still grows. This makes it more of a cash cow than a compounder.

### Financial Fortress Assessment

> **Buffett: "We will reject interesting opportunities rather than over-leverage our balance sheet."**

Swaraj Engines is a **textbook financial fortress**. Key observations:

1. **Zero Debt:** The company has operated with essentially zero borrowings for the entire 12-year period. The ₹1-2 Cr showing in recent years as borrowings is negligible (likely lease liabilities under Ind AS 116). Debt/Equity is 0.00. Debt/EBIT is negligible at 0.01 or less (₹1-2 Cr of borrowings, likely lease liabilities, against ₹207 Cr EBIT). This is as clean as it gets.

2. **Growing Equity Base:** Shareholders' equity has grown from ₹209 Cr to ₹419 Cr (approximately doubling over 12 years), despite paying out 65-91% of earnings as dividends. Book Value Per Share has grown from ₹172 to ₹345 — a CAGR of ~6.5% — which is the residual after massive dividend payouts.

3. **Minimal Fixed Assets:** The company runs a ₹1,682 Cr revenue business on just ₹110 Cr of fixed assets. This asset-light model is incredibly capital efficient and means almost no maintenance capex burden.

4. **Negative Working Capital (Historical):** For most of the period (FY2014-FY2020), the company operated with negative working capital days, meaning suppliers were effectively financing operations. This has shifted to slightly positive working capital in recent years but remains very efficient.

5. **No Goodwill:** There are no acquisitions, no goodwill, no intangible assets of concern. The book value is tangible and real.

6. **Current Ratio at 1.88:** More than adequate for a company with zero debt. The company has plenty of liquidity.

This balance sheet would make Buffett smile. There is **zero financial risk** here. The only risk is operational — and even that is moderated by the captive customer relationship with Mahindra.

## 10. COMPETITIVE POSITION & MOAT ANALYSIS

### Moat Type: Captive Supplier / Switching Cost Moat (Within Mahindra Group)

Swaraj Engines' moat is unusual — it is a **structural, contractual moat** rather than a traditional competitive moat. The company's position as the sole engine supplier for Swaraj tractors creates an effective monopoly within its niche. The switching costs for Mahindra are enormous: changing the engine supplier would require redesigning the entire tractor platform, re-certifying emissions, retraining service networks, and disrupting an integrated supply chain that has worked seamlessly for 35+ years.

### Moat Assessment

**Is the moat enduring or eroding?**

The moat is **enduring but inherently limited**. As long as Swaraj tractors are sold, Swaraj Engines will supply the engines. The moat doesn't expand — it simply persists. The risk is not that someone steals the business; it's that the parent (Mahindra) could theoretically bring engine manufacturing in-house or merge Swaraj Engines entirely. However, given that this arrangement has persisted since 1989 with no sign of change, and that Swaraj Engines has a separate listed entity with minority shareholders, this risk appears low.

**Market Share Dynamics:**

Swaraj tractors hold approximately 10-12% of the Indian tractor market, making it the #3 or #4 brand. The tractor market is dominated by Mahindra (40%+), TAFE/Massey Ferguson (~15%), and Escorts/Kubota. Swaraj's market share has been broadly stable. Since Swaraj Engines' revenue is entirely tied to Swaraj tractor volumes, the relevant question is: Can the Swaraj brand grow its market share?

The answer is nuanced. Revenue has grown at a 10-12% CAGR (10-year basis), outpacing the broader tractor industry volume growth of ~7-8% CAGR. However, Swaraj's market share in unit terms has been **broadly stable at 10-12%** over this period. The ~4-5pp differential between revenue CAGR and industry volume growth is most plausibly explained by **product mix improvement** (shift towards higher-HP engines commanding higher ASPs) and **price increases** (input cost pass-throughs to M&M), not unit market share gains. *(Note: Swaraj Engines does not separately disclose revenue-per-engine or HP-segment mix; this is an inference from industry-level HP demand trends and the absence of unit market share gains.)* This distinction matters — revenue growth driven by mix/price is real but less durable than volume-driven share gains.

**Key Competitors (to the Swaraj tractor brand, not directly to Swaraj Engines):**

- **Mahindra Tractors** — Interestingly, the biggest competitor is within the same group. But M&M has maintained the Swaraj brand as a separate, value-for-money offering vs. Mahindra's premium positioning.
- **TAFE (Massey Ferguson)** — Strong in South India, ~15% share.
- **Escorts Kubota** — Growing brand, strong in North India.
- **Sonalika (International Tractors)** — Aggressive growth, competitive pricing.

None of these pose an existential threat to Swaraj. The tractor market is oligopolistic with established brand loyalties among farmers.

**Technological Disruption Risk:**

**LOW.** Tractors are mechanical workhorses. Electric tractors are decades away from being practical for Indian agriculture (where tractors operate in remote areas for hours). The diesel engine will remain the dominant tractor powertrain for at least 15-20 years. There is no EV disruption imminent in this space.

> **Buffett: "We do not get enthused about change, with a few exceptions, as a way to make a lot of money. We're looking for the absence of change to protect ways that are already making a lot of money and even more in the future."**

This business epitomises Buffett's preference for the **absence of change**. A diesel tractor engine in 2026 functions fundamentally the same as one in 2006 or 1996. The manufacturing process is well-understood. The customer relationship is 35+ years old. The product demand is driven by one of the most enduring economic activities on earth — agriculture. There is no app-driven disruption coming. No cloud migration. No AI pivot needed.

**Debtor Days — A Moat Quality Nuance:** The jump in debtor days from 2-7 (FY2014-FY2020) to 31-42 (FY2021-FY2025) is circumstantial evidence that M&M has begun exercising its financial leverage over the captive supplier — extending payment terms knowing Swaraj Engines has no alternative customer. While this doesn't destroy the moat, it reveals the inherent power asymmetry in a captive-supplier relationship. The moat protects Swaraj's revenue stream, but not necessarily its working capital efficiency. This is a structural limitation of a narrow moat.

### Moat Verdict: MODERATE — Narrow but Durable

The moat is REAL but NARROW. It protects a specific, profitable niche but doesn't have the expansive, self-reinforcing characteristics of a Coca-Cola or Visa moat. It is a captive-supplier moat, which is highly durable but size-limited. The business will persist as long as Swaraj tractors are manufactured — and given Mahindra's commitment to the Swaraj brand, this appears likely for the foreseeable future.

- **SEBI / Stock Exchanges:** Listed company compliance (Swaraj Engines is a listed subsidiary of M&M)

**Current regulatory stance: SUPPORTIVE.** The Indian government actively promotes farm mechanisation, provides subsidies for tractor purchases, and views the tractor industry as a key enabler of agricultural productivity. There are no hostile regulatory actions targeting the sector.
