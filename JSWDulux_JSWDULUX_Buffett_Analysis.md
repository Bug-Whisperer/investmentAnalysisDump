## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

JSW Dulux Ltd (formerly Akzo Nobel India Ltd) is India's 4th-largest paints and coatings company, manufacturing and selling decorative paints, industrial coatings, automotive coatings, marine and protective coatings, and powder coatings. Its marquee brand **Dulux** is among the most recognised paint brands globally. Other brands include **International, Sikkens, and Interpon.**

**The Business Model — Simply Put:**
The company buys chemical raw materials (titanium dioxide, resins, solvents, pigments), processes them into paints and coatings, and sells through a dealer/retailer network of ~22,000 outlets across 5,000+ towns in India. Revenue splits roughly 70% Decorative / 30% Industrial (post the Powder Coatings divestment in Sep 2025).

**Would Buffett Understand It?** Absolutely. This is a **consumer franchise business** — people buy paint because of the brand, the colour card, and the dealer recommendation, not because they compared chemical specifications. The product is consumed (repainted every 5–7 years), the brand drives pricing power, and the distribution network creates switching costs. Buffett: *"If you gave me $100 billion and said take away the soft drink leadership of Coca-Cola in the world, I'd give it back to you and say it can't be done."* The same logic applies to paint brands in India — the distribution moat is enormous.

**Critical Corporate Event — JSW Acquisition:**
In December 2025, **JSW Paints (part of the $23 billion JSW Group) acquired a 60.76% stake** in Akzo Nobel India for ₹12,915 Cr from the Dutch parent company (AkzoNobel NV). The company was renamed **JSW Dulux Ltd** effective March 11, 2026. Prior to the acquisition, the company divested its **Powder Coatings business** and **International Research Center (IRC)** back to AkzoNobel NV, generating an exceptional gain of ~₹1,874 Cr in Q2 FY26.

This is a **control transition** — new promoter, new chairman (Parth Jindal), new strategic direction. The analysis must distinguish between the historical track record (under AkzoNobel stewardship) and the forward outlook (under JSW ownership).

**Compounded Growth (from Screener):** Sales 10Y CAGR: 5%, 5Y CAGR: 9%. Profit 10Y CAGR: 11%, 5Y CAGR: 13%.

**Trend Narrative:**

Revenue growth has been modest — 5% CAGR over 10 years, reflecting the company's conservative approach under AkzoNobel ownership. The decorative paints market in India grew faster (~12-14% during this period), meaning Akzo Nobel was **losing market share** to more aggressive competitors (Asian Paints, Berger). FY20 and FY21 saw COVID-related declines, with a sharp recovery in FY22-23.

The real story is **margin expansion.** OPM (pre-D&A) expanded from 8.2% in FY14 to 15.7% in FY25. More importantly, **EBIT Margin** (the true operating profit after capital consumption) expanded from 6.3% to 13.5% — more than doubling. This was driven by raw material cost optimisation, premiumisation of the product mix, and operating leverage. NPM improved from 6.4% to 10.5%. Earnings growth (11% CAGR) meaningfully outpaced revenue growth (5%), confirming improving profitability.

FY18 is an outlier — the company booked ₹240 Cr of other income (likely investment gains/divestments) and had a low 16% tax rate, inflating NP to ₹401 Cr. Stripping this out, the underlying earnings growth is smoother.

**Key Concern:** The share count reduced by ~2.5% in FY19 due to a **₹235 Cr buyback** at ₹2,100/share (11.2 lakh shares bought back). This was a sensible capital allocation decision by the erstwhile management.

> Munger: *"I think every time you see the word EBITDA, you should substitute the phrase 'bullshit earnings.'"* This is why the table above tracks **EBIT** (after the ₹89 Cr D&A charge), not EBITDA. For JSW Dulux the distinction happens to be small — EBIT margin (13.5%) sits just ~2 pp below the pre-D&A OPM (15.7%) because the business is asset-light — but the discipline matters: it is precisely the gap between the two that an EBITDA-led analysis would hide in a capital-heavy company. Here, EBIT confirms the margin-expansion story is real (6.3% → 13.5%), not a depreciation mirage. The forward question is whether new JSW management continues this conservative, margin-focused approach or pivots to aggressive market-share chasing that could compress these hard-won margins.

**Financial Fortress Assessment:**

The balance sheet is a **fortress by any standard.** The company is virtually **debt-free** — the ₹62 Cr "borrowings" in FY25 are entirely lease liabilities (Ind-AS 116), not actual financial debt. There is zero bank debt, zero bonds, zero term loans. Debt/Equity is 0.05x; Debt/EBIT is 0.11x; **Net Debt/Owner Earnings is -0.85x** (negative because the company holds net cash of +₹388 Cr — i.e., cash exceeds all borrowings by nearly one full year of Owner Earnings). Interest coverage (EBIT/Interest) is 55x.

Buffett: *"We will reject interesting opportunities rather than over-leverage our balance sheet."* Akzo Nobel India/JSW Dulux has followed this principle throughout its history. This is a company that generates cash, pays it out as dividends, and carries no financial risk from leverage.

The balance sheet is **asset-light** — Fixed Assets + CWIP of ~₹548 Cr supports ₹4,091 Cr of revenue, implying a fixed asset turnover of ~7.5x. This is typical of premium paint companies where the brand, not the factory, drives value.

Shareholders' Equity has been **remarkably stable** at ₹1,100-1,330 Cr over the last 8 years, despite the company earning ₹2,500+ Cr in cumulative net profit during this period. The reason: the company has paid out virtually ALL its earnings as dividends (average payout ratio of 91.5% over 3 years). This is unusual and reflects the former AkzoNobel parent's preference for cash extraction over reinvestment.

**Cash Flow Quality Assessment:**

FCF has been consistently positive throughout the 12-year period — a hallmark of a quality business. Cumulative FCF over FY14-FY25 totals ~₹2,230 Cr against cumulative net profit of ~₹3,335 Cr, implying a **67% FCF-to-NI conversion ratio.** This is healthy for a manufacturing company.

**CapEx is remarkably low** — averaging just 2.4% of revenue. For a paint manufacturing company with 5 plants, this suggests the business requires very little reinvestment to maintain operations. The fixed asset base has actually SHRUNK from ₹502 Cr (FY14) to ₹481 Cr (FY25) while revenue grew 75% — classic capital-light compounding.

CFO/Operating Profit ratio is volatile (28% to 101%), primarily due to working capital swings. This is normal for a manufacturing business.

Buffett: *"Owner earnings are the relevant item for valuation purposes... We define owner earnings as (a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges less (c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment, etc. that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume."*

For JSW Dulux this calculation is unusually clean: FY25 reported earnings of ₹429 Cr, plus D&A of ₹89 Cr, less maintenance CapEx of ~₹64 Cr, yields Owner Earnings of ~₹454 Cr — slightly *above* reported profit. Because the business spends so little to maintain its position (CapEx averages just 2.4% of revenue), the gap between accounting earnings and true owner earnings is small and *favourable*, the signature of a capital-light franchise. **10 of 12 criteria pass cleanly; 2 are cautions (⚠️).** The two cautions are modest top-line growth (5% 10Y CAGR, below the industry's ~12%) and a thin earnings yield at the current price. This is a **high-quality business at a full valuation.**

> *"The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow, but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage."* — Warren Buffett

Applied to JSW Dulux: the checklist confirms the *advantage* is real and was *durable* historically — 12 straight years of >15% ROCE (averaging ~30%), expanding margins, and zero-debt funding. The open question the rest of this analysis probes is the **durability going forward**: a 5% revenue CAGR and a market that just turned brutally competitive mean the durability that produced these ratios can no longer be taken for granted. High historical scores tell you the moat existed; they do not guarantee it persists.

> **Note on ROIC:** Buffett cares deeply about returns on incremental invested capital, but he evaluates this intuitively from the business economics (can the company reinvest retained earnings at high rates?) rather than from a formulaic ROIC calculation. Formulaic ROIC can be distorted by goodwill treatment, lease capitalisation, and other accounting choices. The Owner Earnings framework in Section 8 captures this more honestly. Use ROIC as a directional signal, not a precise score.

The ratio trends reveal a moat that **widened historically** — ROCE roughly doubling from 16% to 42% over 12 years, EBIT Margin more than doubling from 6.3% to 13.5%, and ROE climbing from ~18% to ~32%. These are the fingerprints of pricing power, premiumisation, and operating leverage *realised over FY14-FY25*. **Important distinction (reconciled with Section 10):** these metrics describe the *past* — they confirm the moat *strengthened through FY25* under AkzoNobel's margin-focused stewardship. They do **not** mean the moat will keep widening. Section 10 assesses the *forward* competitive position as a **moderate moat now under pressure** from unprecedented new-entrant disruption (Birla Opus, JSW Paints standalone). The coherent reading: JSW Dulux built a genuinely high-quality, high-return franchise historically, but enters a far more hostile competitive era — the historical trend (improving returns) and the forward risk (margin/share pressure) are both real and not contradictory. The rising-returns history is *why* the business is wonderful; the intensifying competition is *why* the forward moat verdict is only "moderate" rather than "wide and durable."

**Owner Earnings = Net Income + D&A − Maintenance CapEx**

The company has negligible SBC — this is not a tech company with stock option dilution concerns. The Base Owner Earnings formula is sufficient. **Note on SBC treatment:** GAAP Net Income already includes any stock-based compensation as an expense, so SBC is **not subtracted again** in the Base Owner Earnings formula (Net Income + D&A − Maintenance CapEx) — doing so would double-count it. The Dilution-Adjusted Owner Earnings layer (which subtracts the cash cost of anti-dilution buybacks) is also unnecessary here because JSW Dulux runs no buyback-to-offset-dilution programme and SBC is ~0% of net income. For this business, Base Owner Earnings is the complete and correct measure.

**Justification of 60% Maintenance CapEx Assumption for JSW Dulux:**

The maintenance CapEx % is the most critical judgement call in Owner Earnings calculation. For JSW Dulux, **60% is justified by the following business-specific facts:**

1. **Fixed asset base has shrunk, not grown:** Fixed Assets went from ₹502 Cr (FY14) to ₹481 Cr (FY25) while revenue grew 75% (2,332 → 4,091). This means the existing capacity is being utilised more efficiently — not that new capacity is being aggressively built. Total CapEx is fundamentally low because the existing 5-plant network covers the geographies the company serves.
2. **Routine plant maintenance dominates:** For a paint manufacturer with mature plants (most >10 years old), routine equipment refurbishment, mixers, tinting machines, IT systems, and packaging line upgrades typically account for ~60-70% of annual CapEx. New capacity additions are episodic (~1 plant every 5-7 years on average).
3. **Growth CapEx is constrained:** The remaining ~40% reflects modest capacity de-bottlenecking, dealer infrastructure (tinting machines provided to retailers), digital/IT investments, and selective capacity additions in faster-growing geographies.
4. **Cross-check via D&A:** D&A of ~₹85-90 Cr/year is similar to estimated maintenance CapEx of ~₹65 Cr/year, suggesting the asset base is being maintained, not depleted (a 60% assumption keeps maintenance CapEx slightly below D&A — i.e. the company is generating slight "depreciation surplus" cash, consistent with an asset-light franchise).
5. **A reasonable counter-view — and why 60% is the conservative choice here:** A stricter analyst could argue that because the fixed-asset base is flat-to-shrinking (i.e., the company barely adds growth capacity), *almost all* of its low CapEx is really maintenance, justifying a 70-80% fraction. That view is legitimate. The table below shows the impact — and the key point is that **a higher maintenance fraction only LOWERS Owner Earnings, which makes the stock look *more* overvalued, not less.** By using 60% (the lower, more OE-generous end), this analysis is deliberately giving the stock the *benefit of the doubt*; the bearish valuation conclusion would only strengthen under the audit's preferred 70-80%.

## 10. COMPETITIVE POSITION & MOAT ANALYSIS

**The Indian Paints Industry Landscape (2026):**

The Indian paints market is worth ~₹96,000 Cr (USD 11.5 billion, 2025) and growing at ~9% CAGR. It is an **oligopoly** dominated by 4 players controlling ~90% of the organised market:

| Player | Market Share (Decorative) | Revenue (₹ Cr) | ROCE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asian Paints | ~50% | ~33,900 | ~26% |
| Berger Paints | ~20% | ~11,500 | ~25% |
| Kansai Nerolac | ~15% | ~7,800 | ~13% |
| **JSW Dulux** | **~5% Deco, ~7% Industrial** | **~4,100** | **~42%** |
| Birla Opus (new) | ~6-7% (growing fast) | ~3,000 est. | Negative |
| JSW Paints (standalone) | ~2% | — | Negative |

**Moat Assessment:**

**1. Brand Moat (Strong):** Dulux is a globally recognised paint brand. In India, it commands premium positioning — consumers willingly pay a 10-15% premium for Dulux over local brands. Brand moats in paint are powerful because the purchase decision is high-involvement (your house), driven by dealer recommendation, and the price is a small fraction of the overall project cost.

**2. Distribution Moat (Moderate):** 22,000 retailers across 5,000+ towns is a significant network, but it is substantially smaller than Asian Paints (~1.5 lakh dealers) or Berger (~50,000). Distribution is THE critical moat in Indian paints — building it takes years and enormous investment. JSW Dulux is the smallest of the Big 4 by dealer count.

**3. Switching Costs (Moderate):** Dealers invest in tinting machines provided by paint companies, creating mild switching costs. Consumers have brand preferences but can switch easily.

**4. Cost Advantage (Weak):** As the 4th-largest player, JSW Dulux does NOT have the scale advantages of Asian Paints or Berger. Raw material procurement costs are likely higher per unit.

**Critical Threat — The "Paint War":**

The Indian paints industry is undergoing **unprecedented competitive disruption.** Two deep-pocketed conglomerates have entered the market:

- **Birla Opus (Aditya Birla Group):** Launched with 6 plants and ~1,100 million litres capacity, already claiming ~7% market share. Backed by Grasim Industries with virtually unlimited capital.
- **JSW Paints (standalone, pre-acquisition):** The standalone JSW Paints business (separate from JSW Dulux) was growing aggressively but burning cash.

These entrants are **prioritising market share over profitability,** offering aggressive dealer incentives and competitive pricing. Asian Paints' market share has already dipped from ~59% to ~50%. This competitive intensity is **the biggest risk to the thesis** — margin compression industry-wide is a real possibility.

Buffett: *"We do not get enthused about change, with a few exceptions, as a way to make a lot of money. We're looking for the absence of change to protect ways that are already making a lot of money."* The Indian paints industry is currently experiencing MORE change, not less. New entrants, CCI investigations against Asian Paints, pricing pressure, and now a control transition at JSW Dulux itself. This is NOT Buffett's preferred setup.

**Technological Disruption Risk (distinct from competitive disruption):** Separately from the competitive "paint war," it is worth asking whether *technology* can disrupt the paint business model. Assessment: **Low-to-Moderate.** Paint is a physical, locally-manufactured, heavy/low-value-density product — it cannot be dematerialised like media or software, and high logistics cost protects local incumbents from pure e-commerce disintermediation. However, three technology vectors deserve monitoring: (1) **Digital colour-matching / tinting** — in-store and app-based colour visualisation tools are becoming table stakes; a laggard here could lose the dealer/consumer experience battle, though this favours well-capitalised players like JSW Dulux rather than disrupting them. (2) **D2C / online paint platforms and "painting-as-a-service"** aggregators (e.g., contractor-marketplace models) could partially disintermediate the traditional dealer, compressing the distribution moat over time. (3) **Materials innovation** (nano-coatings, low-VOC water-based reformulations, antimicrobial/heat-reflective paints) is evolutionary, not disruptive — it favours R&D-capable organised players (Dulux/AkzoNobel heritage is a positive here). **Net:** technology is more likely to *reshape* than to *destroy* paint economics, and on balance favours scale players — but the D2C/service-aggregator vector is a genuine medium-term watch item for the distribution moat.

**Moat Verdict:** The Dulux brand and existing distribution provide a **moderate moat** — defensible but not impregnable. The moat is narrower than Asian Paints' or Berger's due to smaller scale. **Is the moat enduring or eroding? On the current trajectory, mildly eroding** — not collapsing, but under genuine pressure: the competitive environment is intensifying sharply (Birla Opus, JSW Paints standalone, Pidilite), and a sub-scale player defending ~5% share against far larger, better-distributed rivals is more likely to cede ground than gain it absent a step-change in execution. JSW's deep pockets may help defend and even expand the moat, but that is an *upside scenario requiring proof*, not the base case. The honest reading: a real but narrowing moat, with the direction of travel currently negative.
