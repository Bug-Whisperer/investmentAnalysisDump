## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

Shreyans Industries Ltd (SIL), incorporated in 1979, is a writing and printing paper manufacturer promoted by the Oswal family of Ludhiana. The company operates two paper manufacturing units at Ahmedgarh (Dist. Sangrur) and Banah (Dist. Nawanshaher) in Punjab, with a combined installed capacity of approximately 37,000 metric tonnes per annum. SIL manufactures paper from agricultural residues — wheat straw, bagasse, and sarkanda — a distinctive feature that reduces dependence on wood pulp.

**Products:** High brightness paper, cream wove, coloured paper, duplicating paper, maplitho paper, stamp paper, railway ticket paper, postal envelope paper, offset paper, and super printing paper in grades ranging from 44 GSM to 200 GSM.

**Customers:** State textbook boards (government tender business), publishers, copy manufacturers, printing & stationery departments, Indian Railways, P&T department, security presses, and exporters of notebooks and diaries. SIL operates one marketing branch in Delhi with approximately 100 dealers nationwide.

**Business Classification: Commodity.** Paper is a commodity product where pricing power is limited and margins are driven by the cost of raw materials (chemicals, energy, agro-residues) and supply-demand dynamics in the paper market. There are no meaningful switching costs — a publisher can switch between paper suppliers without friction. There is no brand premium — the end consumer neither knows nor cares which mill produced the paper in their notebook.

Buffett: *"When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."* Paper manufacturing is a tough business — capital-intensive, cyclical, commodity-priced, and subject to raw material volatility. However, SIL has a niche advantage: its agro-residue based production gives it a structural cost advantage in a raw material abundant region (Punjab), and its products serve a relatively stable demand base (education, government stationery). The business is simple enough for Buffett to understand — buy raw materials, make paper, sell it — but the economics are challenging.

**Key Observation:** SIL's market value of investments (₹240 Cr) exceeds its entire market capitalisation (₹238 Cr). This means the market is assigning approximately ZERO value to the operating paper business and all its assets — or alternatively, the investment portfolio alone justifies the current market price. This anomaly is central to the valuation thesis.

> *Note on Operating Profit / EBIT source:* Screener.in's "Operating Profit" is calculated before deducting Depreciation & Amortisation, making it EBITDA-equivalent. This analysis tracks **EBIT (Operating Profit minus D&A)** as the primary operating profitability metric per Buffett/Munger philosophy. Buffett (2000 Letter): *"References to EBITDA make us shudder — does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?"* Munger: *"I think every time you see the word EBITDA, you should substitute the phrase 'bullshit earnings.'"* EBIT captures the true operating profit after accounting for the capital the business consumes. The identity **Operating Profit (pre-D&A) = EBIT + D&A** holds for every year above, confirming Screener's Operating Profit is correctly placed in the pre-D&A row (not double-counting D&A).

> *Note on EBIT < Net Income in FY20, FY21, FY22, FY24 and the TTM period:* In these periods EBIT is below Net Income — but this is **not** a data error or D&A double-subtraction. It is explained entirely by SIL's large **Other Income** (investment portfolio income of ₹6–35 Cr), which flows into Net Income but sits below the EBIT line. In FY21-22, the operating business produced negative EBIT (paper-cycle trough) while ₹20 Cr of annual investment income kept Net Income positive. In FY24, ₹35 Cr of Other Income lifted NI (₹87 Cr) above EBIT (₹84 Cr); likewise in the TTM period ₹20 Cr of Other Income lifts NI (₹35 Cr) above EBIT (₹29 Cr). This is a genuine structural feature of a company whose investment portfolio (₹240 Cr) generates substantial non-operating income — see Section 3.

A reliable Gross Profit / Gross Margin cannot therefore be computed from the available data without fabricating a COGS split, so these two template rows are intentionally omitted rather than estimated. OPM (pre-D&A) and EBIT Margin (both shown above) capture profitability at the operating level. For a paper manufacturer, raw materials (agro-residue, chemicals) plus power & fuel are the dominant variable costs.

**Trend Narrative:**

Revenue is highly cyclical and has grown at a modest 4% CAGR over 11 years (FY14-FY25), reflecting the commodity nature of paper pricing. The peak year was FY23 (₹866 Cr) driven by post-COVID paper price spikes, followed by a sharp normalisation. Revenue has now settled in the ₹617-634 Cr range.

Margins are equally cyclical. OPM (pre-D&A) swung from a trough of just 1% in FY21 (COVID disruption) to a peak of 14% (reached in FY17, FY19 and again FY24), before compressing to 11% in FY25 and further to ~7% in the TTM period. **EBIT Margin** — the true profitability metric after accounting for capital consumption — tells a starker story: it swung from -1.6% in FY21 to a peak of 12.4% in FY19 (with FY24 a close second at 12.0%), but has now compressed to 4.6% TTM. The gap between OPM (7%) and EBIT Margin (4.6%) in the TTM period reflects the ₹16 Cr annual depreciation charge — a real cost of maintaining the paper mills that EBITDA-based analysis would hide. This volatility is characteristic of commodity businesses — when paper prices rise and raw material costs lag, margins expand dramatically; when the cycle reverses, margins compress just as violently.

Net profit growth has massively outpaced revenue growth over 11 years (13.2% CAGR vs. 4.0%), reflecting significant margin expansion from the FY14-16 trough. However, this is not structural improvement — it is cyclical. The business alternates between periods of excellent profitability (FY17-19, FY23-24) and near-zero earnings (FY21-22).

**Zero dilution:** Share count has remained absolutely constant at 1.40 Cr throughout (per Equity Capital ÷ Face Value convention). No equity raises, no ESOPs, no dilution. This is a significant positive — every rupee of retained earnings accrues fully to existing shareholders.

**Buffett's "Financial Fortress" Assessment:**

Buffett: *"We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet tomorrow's obligations... We will arrange our affairs so that any requirements for cash we may conceivably have will be dwarfed by our own liquidity."* SIL embodies this — ₹240 Cr of liquid investments against just ₹45 Cr of debt means the company is never dependent on creditors or capital markets to survive a downturn. This is the financial fortress that lets a cyclical business wait out troughs (like the current Dec25 operating loss) without distress.

**The Investment Portfolio — The Hidden Treasure:** The most striking feature is the investment portfolio, which has grown from ₹17 Cr in FY14 to ₹240 Cr in FY25 — a 27% CAGR. This portfolio now represents 39% of total assets and exceeds the company's market capitalisation. The company has been systematically investing its surplus cash flows into this portfolio rather than over-expanding the paper business. This is exceptionally rational capital allocation for a cyclical business — when paper margins are high, SIL harvests cash and parks it in investments; when margins compress, the investment income provides a cushion.

Book Value Per Share has compounded from ₹60.71 to ₹305.00 over 11 years — a CAGR of 15.8%. Despite this, the stock trades at 0.56x book. The market is pricing the operating business at approximately negative value.

The maintenance CapEx proportion must be justified for THIS specific business. 1. **Maintenance ≈ Depreciation:** D&A (~₹15 Cr in FY25) is the accountant's estimate of annual asset consumption. Under this view, Base Owner Earnings = Net Income + D&A − Maintenance CapEx ≈ Net Income.

Because total reported CapEx is larger than pure steady-state maintenance CapEx, applying 70% to the *full* stream is consistent with the 75-90% maintenance share of *normalised* CapEx. As a reasonableness check, 70% of FY25 CapEx (₹21 Cr) already *exceeds* book depreciation (₹15 Cr), accounting for replacement-cost inflation. The remaining 30% is treated as growth/discretionary.

The valuation conclusion is robust to this assumption; the asset-NAV anchor (₹304) is unaffected by the maintenance-CapEx choice entirely.

## 10. COMPETITIVE POSITION & MOAT ANALYSIS

**Moat Type: Cost Advantage (Narrow) + Niche Position (Weak)**

SIL operates in the commodity paper segment. Its primary competitive advantages are:

1. **Agro-residue based production:** Using wheat straw, bagasse, and sarkanda as raw materials instead of imported wood pulp gives a structural cost advantage in Punjab, where these agricultural residues are abundantly available. This advantage is regional and tied to proximity to raw material sources.

2. **Two-unit operational flexibility:** Having two plants at different locations provides some operational resilience and flexibility to optimise production based on local raw material availability.

3. **Established government relationships:** Decades of supplying to state textbook boards, railways, postal department, and security presses creates some incumbency advantage, though government contracts are ultimately tender-based and price-competitive.

**Moat Assessment — Honest Evaluation:**

The moat is **narrow and eroding**, not wide and enduring. Here's why:

- **No brand premium:** Paper is paper. SIL cannot charge more than competitors for the same grade.
- **No switching costs:** Any printer or publisher can switch paper suppliers with zero friction.
- **Limited pricing power:** Paper prices are set by supply-demand dynamics, not by individual producers. When industry capacity exceeds demand, prices and margins collapse (as visible in FY20-22 and the current Dec25 quarter).
- **No network effects:** More customers don't make the product more valuable.

**Key Competitors:** JK Paper, Tamil Nadu Newsprint, West Coast Paper, Star Paper Mills, Kuantum Papers, Emami Paper. JK Paper is the dominant player with far larger scale, wood-based production, and integrated operations. SIL is a small player in a fragmented industry.

**Market Share Trajectory:** SIL holds roughly 1.5-2% of India's ₹30,000-40,000 Cr W&P paper market — a share that has been broadly **stable-to-slightly-declining** over the analysis period. With capacity fixed at ~37,000 MT while the overall market grows ~4-5% annually, SIL's relative share gently erodes over time unless it expands capacity (which it has chosen not to do, preferring to redeploy cash into its investment portfolio). This is consistent with the "narrow, not widening" moat assessment — SIL defends its niche but is not gaining ground on larger, integrated players.

**Technological Disruption Risk:** The long-term trend toward digitalisation is a headwind for writing and printing paper. Textbooks are increasingly available digitally, official communications are going paperless, and e-commerce reduces the need for printed catalogues. However, India's large and young population, growing education system, and government examination infrastructure continue to drive paper demand. The disruption risk is real but gradual — not an imminent threat over a 5-10 year horizon.

**Susceptibility to Change:** Buffett said, *"We're looking for the absence of change to protect ways that are already making a lot of money."* Paper manufacturing has been largely unchanged for decades — the same machines, same processes, same products. This stability is a positive from Buffett's perspective. However, the DEMAND landscape IS changing (digital substitution), which introduces uncertainty.

## 11. SCALABILITY CHECK — Can This Business Scale and Is It Scaling?

### Part A: Unit Economics of Scaling

**1. Revenue Scalability — Can revenue grow without proportional resource additions?**

| Factor | Assessment for SIL | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal cost structure | Each additional tonne of paper requires proportional raw materials (agro-residue, chemicals), power, and machine time. No near-zero marginal cost. | ❌ Unfavourable |
| Pricing power at scale | Paper is a commodity; prices set by industry supply-demand, not by SIL. Scale brings no pricing power. | ❌ Unfavourable |
| Distribution leverage | Existing dealer network (~100 dealers) and government-tender channels can carry incremental volume at low cost, but volume is capped by plant capacity. | ⚠️ Neutral |

**Revenue scalability is poor.** Each additional tonne requires proportional inputs and there is no marginal-cost or pricing-power advantage. Revenue growth requires either capacity expansion (capital-intensive) or higher paper prices (commodity-cycle-driven).

## 21. REGULATORY RISKS & POLICY HEADWINDS

**Key Regulatory Bodies:** CPCB (Central Pollution Control Board), SPCBs (State Pollution Control Boards), MOEFCC (Ministry of Environment), and various state governments.

**Current Regulatory Environment:** Neutral to mildly positive. The Indian government has been encouraging domestic paper manufacturing to reduce imports, and the ban on single-use plastics creates some incremental demand for paper packaging.

| Event | Year | Nature | Impact on SIL |
|---|---|---|---|

| Environment norms tightening | Ongoing | Stricter emission standards | Manageable — SIL already has chemical recovery plants; smaller competitors face higher compliance costs (moat-strengthening) |

**Current Headwind Analysis — 6-Point Framework:**

The most relevant current/imminent headwind is the combination of (i) rising environmental-compliance requirements and (ii) the secular digital-substitution trend. Assessing both through the prescribed 6-point lens:

1. **Nature and Magnitude:** Mild-to-moderate and gradual. Environmental norms tighten incrementally (effluent/emission standards); digital substitution erodes W&P paper demand slowly. Neither is a sudden shock.
2. **Demand/Volume Impact:** Environmental norms have negligible demand impact (they raise costs, not curb demand). Digital substitution gradually shrinks the addressable W&P market — perhaps low-single-digit % per year long-term — but India's expanding education base and government/examination paper demand offset much of this in the near-to-medium term.
3. **Revenue Impact:** Minimal near-term. SIL can pass through modest compliance costs via paper pricing during normal cycles. Revenue is far more sensitive to the *commodity paper-price cycle* than to regulation.
4. **Margin Impact:** Environmental compliance could compress margins 1-3 pp in a stress scenario (higher chemical/effluent-treatment costs), most painful during a trough like the current one. SIL's existing chemical-recovery plants limit this versus sub-scale peers.
5. **Competitive Impact — moat-relevant:** **Environmental tightening actually STRENGTHENS SIL's relative position.** Compliance is a fixed cost that sub-scale, non-compliant mills cannot easily afford — every tightening cycle pushes marginal competitors toward closure, consolidating volume toward compliant players like SIL. This is the paradox the market often misses.
6. **Structural vs. Cyclical:** Environmental compliance is a manageable, absorbable cost (cyclical/ongoing, not impairing). Digital substitution is the one genuinely *structural* force — but it plays out over 10-20 years, not over the investment horizon, and is partially offset by India's demographic/education tailwinds.

**Demand / Regulatory Elasticity — How sensitive is SIL's business to regulatory change?**

| Scenario | Trigger | Likely Demand/Margin Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild** | Routine tightening of effluent/emission norms | Low — SIL's existing chemical-recovery and pollution-control infrastructure already meets most norms; incremental compliance capex absorbed in normal CapEx. May *help* relative position vs. sub-scale mills. |
| **Moderate** | Sharp rise in environmental compliance costs or restrictions on agro-residue burning/sourcing | Medium — could raise input/compliance costs 5-10%, compressing already-thin margins in a trough; manageable but unwelcome. |

**Buffett's Three-Lens Framework:**

1. **History:** Regulatory events (demonetisation, GST, COVID, environmental norms) have caused *temporary* disruption but never permanent impairment — SIL recovered from each. Environmental tightening has, if anything, hurt smaller competitors more.
2. **Elasticity:** Demand for W&P paper is *moderately* inelastic to regulation in the near term (education and government stationery are non-discretionary) but *elastic* to the secular digital-substitution trend over the long term.
3. **Terminal Risk:** The only regulatory/structural force capable of permanently impairing the business is digital substitution — and even that operates on a multi-decade timeline in India given the education/examination infrastructure. No imminent terminal regulatory threat.
