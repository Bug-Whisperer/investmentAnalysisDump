## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

State Bank of India is India's largest bank by every conceivable measure — assets, deposits, branches, customers, and market capitalisation within the PSU banking universe. With over 200 years of history, 22,000+ branches, and a deposit market share of ~22.5%, SBI is not merely a bank — it is India's financial infrastructure.

**Would Buffett understand it?** Absolutely. Banking is one of Buffett's favourite sectors — he owned significant stakes in Wells Fargo, Bank of America, and American Express for decades. A bank takes deposits, lends money, and earns the spread. SBI does this at a scale no other Indian institution matches.

**Business Model Type:** SBI is a **toll bridge** on India's financial system. Every rupee that flows through India's economy — from government salary disbursements to home loans to corporate credit — disproportionately flows through SBI's pipes. When the Indian economy grows, SBI grows almost by definition.

> *Buffett: "I try to invest in businesses that are so wonderful that an idiot can run them. Because sooner or later, one will."* — SBI has survived countless management changes (being government-run), multiple credit crises, and India's volatile economic cycles. The franchise is bigger than any individual manager.

**Type of Financial Institution:** Universal Bank (Public Sector) — the largest in India. Operates across retail banking, corporate banking, treasury, insurance (SBI Life, SBI General), asset management (SBI MF — India's largest), and international operations across 30+ countries.

**Core Economic Engine:** Primarily spread income (NIM-driven), supplemented by significant fee income from cross-selling insurance, mutual funds, and transaction banking. The primary moat is **cheap funding** — SBI's massive CASA (Current Account Savings Account) deposit base gives it structurally lower cost of funds than virtually every competitor.

**Critical Buffett Insight for Banks:** For SBI, deposits are the raw material of the business, not a financing choice. The ₹54+ lakh crore deposit base is simultaneously a liability (owed to depositors) AND the bank's single greatest competitive asset (cheap funding that generates NIM). This fundamentally changes how every financial metric must be interpreted.

### Trend Narrative

The income statement tells a dramatic turnaround story. SBI went through a devastating NPA cycle during FY2016-2019, with the nadir in FY2018 when the bank posted a net loss of ₹3,749 Cr — its worst year. The RBI's Asset Quality Review (AQR) forced recognition of hidden bad loans, leading to massive provisioning that wiped out profits.

From that trough, SBI has executed one of the most impressive bank turnarounds in Indian history:
- Net profit went from **-₹3,749 Cr (FY2018)** to **₹80,523 Cr (FY2025)** — a transformation from loss-making to India's most profitable bank
- EPS went from **-₹5.11 to ₹86.91** in 7 years
- ROE recovered from **-2% to 17%** — now solidly in the "Good to Excellent" tier
- Revenue growth has been steady at 9-19% CAGR over various periods

**Key concerns to watch:**
- The 5-year consolidated profit CAGR of ~31% (FY2020 ₹21,140 Cr → FY2025 ₹80,523 Cr) is spectacular but is heavily base-effected (starting from the NPA crisis trough). Going forward, profit growth will normalise to 12-15%.
- Other Income at ₹1,99,771 Cr (TTM) is very significant — driven by subsidiary income (SBI Life, SBI MF), treasury gains, and fee income. This diversification is a strength.

> *Buffett: "In business, I look for economic castles protected by unbreachable moats."* — SBI's ₹54+ lakh crore deposit base IS the moat. No competitor can replicate this overnight.

### Buffett's Financial Fortress Assessment — Adapted for Banks

**1. Capital Adequacy:** SBI's CRAR (Capital Adequacy Ratio) stands at approximately 13.5-14% with CET-1 at ~10-11%. While above the RBI minimum of 11.5% (including buffers), it is not as strong as some private banks (which operate at 16-18%). This is adequate but not a fortress. The recent equity raise (FY26, bringing shares from 892 Cr to 923 Cr) indicates the bank needs occasional capital top-ups for growth. **Score: Adequate but not elite.**

**2. Asset Quality:** This is where SBI truly shines in the current cycle. GNPA has fallen from a horrific **10.91% (FY2018) to 2.13% (FY2025)**. Net NPA is at just **0.52%** with a Provision Coverage Ratio of ~93% — meaning almost all bad loans are already provided for. The balance sheet is CLEAN. **Score: Excellent — currently at multi-decade best.**

**3. Funding Stability:** SBI has the most stable funding base of any Indian bank. With ₹54+ lakh crore in deposits, a CASA ratio of ~41-43%, and the majority being granular retail deposits from 50+ crore customers, the deposit base is extremely sticky. No wholesale funding crisis can threaten SBI. The Government of India guarantee (implicit) further eliminates any run-on-the-bank risk. **Score: Best-in-class.**

**4. Hidden Risks:** The restructured book has been largely absorbed post-COVID. Slippages have moderated significantly. The SMA (Special Mention Account) pool is manageable. There is no visible large hidden NPA risk at this stage of the cycle. **However** — and this is the critical Buffett caveat — this is a benign credit cycle. The true test comes during the next downturn.

> *Buffett: "When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."* — For banks, bad economics = bad asset quality. SBI's asset quality is currently excellent, but investors must remember that one credit cycle can destroy a decade of profits. SBI's 2016-2019 experience is proof. The key question is: has the bank's risk management structurally improved, or are we simply enjoying a cyclical sweet spot?

## 4. CASH FLOW ANALYSIS — Earnings Quality & Capital Generation

> **Critical Note:** Traditional cash flow analysis is meaningless for banks and NBFCs. When a bank grows its loan book, that shows up as massive cash outflow from operations — but that is the equivalent of a retailer buying inventory, not a sign of cash burn. Free Cash Flow (CFO minus CapEx) is a nonsensical metric for a bank. SBI's wildly fluctuating operating cash flows (ranging from -₹96,508 Cr in FY2018 to +₹89,919 Cr in FY2021) reflect loan book growth patterns, not operating health.

### Analysis

SBI's internal capital generation rate has improved dramatically — from ~7% in FY2020 to ~14% in FY2025. This means the bank is generating enough retained earnings to grow its book value by ~14% annually WITHOUT external equity raises. At this rate, BVPS should compound from ~₹546 (FY2025) to ~₹1,050-1,100 in 5 years — which means at the current price of ₹1,059, you are buying today at roughly what the book value will be in 5 years.

**Equity Dilution History:**
- FY2018: Equity rose from ₹797 Cr to ₹892 Cr (rights issue/QIP to recapitalise during NPA crisis) — ~12% dilution at depressed prices. **Value-destructive** for existing shareholders.
- FY2026 (H1): Equity rose from ₹892 Cr to ₹923 Cr (~3.5% dilution) — likely a QIP at elevated prices (around ₹750-800 per share given it was before the recent rally). **Mildly accretive** as raised above book value.

> *Buffett: "A bank that must repeatedly raise equity capital to grow is destroying per-share value."* — SBI's history includes dilutive equity raises during the crisis (bad), but the current internal capital generation rate of ~14% suggests that frequent dilution is no longer necessary for normal growth. The FY26 raise was modest and likely timed to build a capital buffer, not to survive.

## 8. CAPEX QUALITY ANALYSIS — Earnings Quality & Provision Adequacy Analysis [BANK MODE]

For banks, the equivalent of "maintenance CapEx" is credit cost (provisions). Just as a factory must spend to maintain its equipment, a bank must provision to maintain the quality of its loan book.

### Normalised Earnings ("Bank Owner Earnings") — ₹ Crores

| Metric | FY2020 | FY2021 | FY2022 | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net Income | 21,140 | 23,888 | 37,183 | 57,750 | 69,543 | 80,523 |
| Add Back: D&A | 3,662 | 3,711 | 3,691 | 3,696 | 3,849 | 3,991 |
| Actual Provisions (Est.) | ~48,000 | ~52,000 | ~38,000 | ~23,000 | ~18,000 | ~16,000 |
| 5-Year Avg Credit Cost (% of Avg Advances) | ~1.5% | ~1.4% | ~1.3% | ~1.1% | ~0.9% | ~0.7% |
| Normalised Credit Cost (5Y avg applied to current advances) | ~38,000 | ~38,500 | ~36,000 | ~35,000 | ~36,000 | ~35,000 |
| Over/(Under) Provisioning | +10,000 | +13,500 | +2,000 | -12,000 | -18,000 | -19,000 |

| **Normalised Earnings (Owner Earnings)** | ~15,000 | ~17,000 | ~32,000 | ~50,000 | ~56,000 | ~65,000 |
| **Normalised EPS (₹)** | ~16.8 | ~19.1 | ~35.9 | ~56.1 | ~62.8 | ~72.9 |

*(Methodology note: For FY2023-FY2025, Normalised Earnings are computed as: Net Income + D&A + Over/(Under) Provisioning adjustment, where Over/(Under) = Actual Provisions − Normalised Credit Cost. This effectively replaces actual provisions with normalised provisions: e.g., FY2025 = ₹80,523 + ₹3,991 + (−₹19,000) = ~₹65,500 Cr. For FY2020-FY2022, the Over/(Under) row shows the bank was over-provisioning relative to the rolling 5-year average, but the bottom-line normalised earnings for those years are independently estimated lower-bound figures — NOT mechanically derived from the Over/(Under) row. During the NPA supercycle tail, even "normalised" provisioning would have been elevated due to the stressed starting book, so a simple rolling-average adjustment overstates sustainable earnings for those years. The FY2025 figure of ~₹65,000 Cr / ~₹73 EPS is the key number used for all forward-looking valuation.)*

### Analysis

**Critical Finding:** In FY2020-FY2022, SBI was provisioning ABOVE normalised levels — it was being conservative, cleaning up legacy NPAs. This means reported earnings were BELOW true sustainable earnings during those years.

Since FY2023, however, the actual credit cost has fallen BELOW the historical 5-year average. This means current reported earnings are HIGHER than normalised "owner earnings." The gap is about ₹15,000-19,000 Cr — meaning roughly 20-25% of reported FY2025 profits come from lower-than-normalised provisions.

**This does NOT mean the bank is under-provisioning.** The PCR at ~93% is very high, and the GNPA at 2.13% is genuinely low. What it means is that when the credit cycle eventually turns — and it will — provisions will rise, and reported earnings will fall back toward the normalised level.

**Normalised EPS for valuation purposes:** ~₹73 (not the reported ₹87-92). This gives a normalised PE of ~14.5x at ₹1,059, compared to the reported PE of ~12x. The stock is not as cheap as it looks on headline PE.

> *Buffett on the difference between accounting earnings and economic earnings: "Accounting is the language of business... but it can also obscure the truth."* — For SBI, the truth is that ~₹73/share of EPS is sustainable through a normal credit cycle. The extra ₹15-18/share is cyclical tailwind from benign credit conditions.

**⚠️ Credit Cost Sensitivity — The Most Important Assumption in This Analysis:**

The normalised credit cost above uses a recovery-era window (FY2021-2025 average ~0.9%). This is the optimistic end of a range. Full-cycle normalisation tells a very different story:

| Normalisation Window | Avg Credit Cost | Norm. Provisions (₹ Cr) | Norm. Earnings (₹ Cr) | Norm. EPS | Implied Fair Value (14.5x PE) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Recovery-era (FY2021-2025)** | **~0.9%** | **~35,000** | **~65,000** | **~₹73** | **~₹1,059 (≈ CMP)** |
| Buffett conservative (1.2%) | ~1.2% | ~46,000 | ~54,000 | ~₹60 | ~₹870 |
| Mid-cycle (FY2018-2025) | ~1.5% | ~57,000 | ~43,000 | ~₹48 | ~₹696 |
| Full-cycle (FY2016-2025) | ~2.0% | ~76,000 | ~24,000 | ~₹27 | ~₹391 |

The analysis uses ₹73 (recovery-era) as the primary normalised EPS. If a more conservative 1.2% credit cost is applied (roughly midway between the current benign 0.4% and the 2.0% full-cycle average), normalised EPS falls to ~₹60 and fair value to ~₹870 — implying the stock is **moderately overvalued at ₹1,059**. This sensitivity is the single most important variable for the investment decision. The "Good Bank at a Fair Price" verdict holds only under recovery-era normalisation; under full-cycle normalisation, SBI appears overvalued.

## 10. COMPETITIVE POSITION & MOAT ANALYSIS

### Moat Type: **Cost Advantage (Cheap Deposits) + Distribution Scale + Regulatory Moat**

SBI's moat is multi-layered and among the widest in Indian banking:

**1. CASA Ratio & Cost of Deposits (Primary Moat — Cheap Funding):**
SBI's CASA ratio of ~41-43% translates to a cost of deposits of approximately 4.5-5.0%, among the lowest in the industry. With ₹54 lakh crore in deposits — 22.5% of India's entire banking system — this is an unassailable funding advantage. Every quarter, every year, SBI starts the game with cheaper raw material than almost every competitor. This flows directly to NIM and profitability.

**2. Distribution & Branch Network:**
22,000+ branches across India, including deep rural penetration. This physical network took over 200 years to build. The closest competitor (Bank of Baroda) has less than half the branches. Digital layering (YONO app with 7+ crore users) adds a modern acquisition channel on top of this legacy distribution.

**3. Underwriting Skill (Improving):**
SBI's through-cycle NPA performance is mixed — it was badly hit during the 2016-2019 crisis. However, the bank has dramatically improved its risk management since. Post-COVID, NPA management has been excellent. The test will be the NEXT downturn.

**4. Regulatory Moat:**
As India's largest PSU bank and a designated systemically important financial institution (D-SIFI), SBI enjoys implicit sovereign backing. No depositor will ever lose money. New banking licenses are extremely scarce (RBI has issued only 2 universal bank licenses in the last 20 years). This creates a near-impenetrable entry barrier.

**5. Cross-Sell Platform:**
SBI Life Insurance (~56% stake), SBI Mutual Fund (India's largest by AUM), SBI Cards (~69% stake) — these subsidiaries generate significant fee income and are sold through the bank's massive distribution network. The value of the platform is in the cross-sell ability.

**6. Technology & Digital:**
SBI has invested heavily in digital (YONO platform). Digital transactions as a percentage of total are rising. However, SBI is NOT a technology leader — it is a fast follower.

**7. Fintech Disruption Risk:**
UPI has reduced some fee income potential, and digital lenders compete at the margin. However, for a bank of SBI's scale — where the moat is primarily cheap deposits, not technology — fintech is a manageable threat, not an existential one. Fintechs don't take deposits. They are asset-side competitors only, and even there, they cannot match the pricing of a bank with 41% CASA.

### Key Competitors and Threat Assessment

| Competitor | Threat Level | Nature of Threat |
|---|---|---|
| HDFC Bank | Manageable | Better technology, better ROA, but SBI's cost-of-deposits advantage narrows the gap; HDFC is focused on different customer segments |
| ICICI Bank | Manageable | Strong retail franchise; competes on similar products but lacks SBI's deposit scale |
| Bank of Baroda / PNB | Low | Smaller PSU peers; SBI dominates within the PSU space |
| Kotak / Axis | Low-Moderate | Private bank competitors with niche strengths but fraction of SBI's scale |
| Fintech (Paytm, PhonePe, etc.) | Low | Cannot take deposits; compete on payments (low margin) and small-ticket lending (high risk) |

### Susceptibility to Change — Buffett's "Absence of Change" Test

> *Buffett: "We do not get enthused about change... we're looking for the absence of change to protect ways that are already making a lot of money."*

Banking in India is NOT a rapidly changing business at its core. People will still need savings accounts, home loans, car loans, and credit facilities in 10 and 20 years. The delivery mechanism may evolve (digital vs. branch), but the fundamental business — take deposits, make loans, earn the spread — will remain identical.

SBI's moat (cheap deposits, branch network, government backing) is particularly resistant to change. The deposit base is sticky, the branch network is built, and the regulatory moat protects against new entrants. **Verdict: SBI passes Buffett's "absence of change" test.**

## 17. ROE QUALITY CHECK & RETURN EXPECTATION FRAMEWORK

### ROCE vs. ROE — Bank-Mode Inapplicability Note

> For banks, ROCE (Return on Capital Employed) is not a meaningful metric because there is no clean separation between "operating capital" and "financing capital" — deposits are simultaneously the bank's primary liability AND its core operating asset. ROIC faces the same issue. For this reason, the compliance template's ROCE > ROE quality check is replaced with the **ROA > 1% quality check** (which tests whether the bank earns genuinely on its asset base) and the **ROA × Leverage = ROE decomposition** (which tests whether high ROE comes from operational quality or financial gearing). See below.

### Part A: Bank-Specific ROE Decomposition (Quality vs. Leverage)

| Component | FY2021 | FY2022 | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ROA | 0.5% | 0.7% | 1.0% | 1.1% | **1.15%** |
| Leverage (Assets/Equity) | 17.6x | 17.5x | 16.6x | 16.2x | **15.0x** |
| **ROE = ROA × Leverage** | **9%** | **12%** | **17%** | **17%** | **17%** |

**Quality Check:**
- ROE has risen from 9% to 17% — entirely driven by ROA improvement (0.5% to 1.15%), NOT by leverage increase.
- In fact, leverage has DECREASED from 17.6x to 15.0x — making the ROE improvement even more impressive.
- This is exactly what Buffett looks for: genuine quality improvement, not financial engineering.

**Is ROE being inflated by low provisions?**
- Current credit cost (~0.3%) is well below the 5-year average (~0.9%). This means current reported ROE of 17% is cyclically elevated.
- Normalised ROE (using normalised credit cost) is approximately **14-15%** — still comfortably above cost of equity.
- *(Note on credit cost figures: The ~0.3% credit cost shown in Sections 5A/9/13 is sourced from Screener's ratio page. Back-computing from actual provisions (~₹16,000 Cr) on ~₹38 lakh Cr advances yields ~0.42%. The discrepancy likely reflects different advance-base denominators (gross vs net, average vs closing). The normalised earnings calculation in Section 8 uses absolute ₹ figures, not percentages, ensuring internal consistency.)*

**Verdict: Current ROE is ~2-3% above sustainable levels due to benign credit cycle, but the underlying ROA improvement from 0.5% to ~0.9-1.0% is REAL and structural.**

## 21. REGULATORY RISKS & POLICY HEADWINDS — Industry-Specific Assessment [BANK/NBFC MODE]

> **Purpose:** Every bank operates under the constant shadow of regulatory action. Buffett pays close attention to regulatory risk because it can permanently impair economics — or, paradoxically, it can STRENGTHEN moats by raising barriers to entry.

### The Core Issue:

SBI operates under the supervision of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), SEBI (for listed entity compliance), and the Department of Financial Services (DFS) under the Ministry of Finance. The regulatory stance toward Indian banking has been **broadly supportive** since 2015 — the RBI's Asset Quality Review (AQR), while painful short-term, ultimately cleaned up the banking system and created a healthier foundation for the current profit cycle.

### Historical Regulatory Impact:

| Event | Year | Nature of Regulation | Impact on SBI |
|---|---|---|---|
| RBI Asset Quality Review (AQR) | 2015-16 | Forced NPA recognition | GNPA spiked from ~5% to ~10.9%; short-term P&L devastation; long-term cleansing |
| IBC / NCLT framework | 2016 | Resolution mechanism for stressed assets | Enabled recovery on large NPAs; SBI recovered thousands of crores from Essar Steel, Bhushan Steel etc. |
| Demonetisation | Nov 2016 | ₹500/1000 note withdrawal | Short-term disruption; massive CASA surge benefited SBI's deposit franchise |
| Farm Loan Waivers | 2017-19 | State-level directed waivers | ~₹5,000 Cr impact for SBI; manageable given scale |
| COVID Moratorium / Restructuring | 2020-21 | RBI-directed moratorium | Temporary NIM compression; restructured book peaked at ~1.5% then resolved cleanly |
| Digital Lending Norms | 2022-23 | RBI tightened digital lending framework | Minimal direct impact on SBI (primarily brick-and-mortar); indirectly beneficial by constraining fintech competitors |
| RBI Rate Hike Cycle | 2022-24 | Repo rate +250bps | NIM expanded as asset repricing led deposit repricing; now reversing |
| Expected Rate Cut Cycle | 2025-26 | Repo rate cuts beginning | NIM compression expected; partially offset by lower deposit costs (lagging) |

### Current Regulatory Headwind Analysis:

**1. Interest Rate Cycle Reversal (Moderate Impact):** The RBI has begun cutting rates, which will compress NIM by 10-30bps over the next 12-18 months. SBI is mildly liability-sensitive — deposit repricing lags asset repricing — so the initial impact of rate cuts compresses NIM before deposit costs adjust downward.

**2. CASA Erosion / Deposit Competition:** As rates fall, CASA ratios tend to improve (savings become relatively more attractive vs. term deposits). However, competition from small finance banks, payment banks, and high-yield fintech products creates structural pressure on deposit costs.

**3. Priority Sector Lending (PSL):** SBI must lend 40% of adjusted net bank credit to priority sectors (agriculture, MSMEs, housing, education). This constrains lending flexibility and often carries lower yields. However, SBI has historically met PSL targets without significant stress.

### The Demand Curve — How Price-Sensitive Is Banking to Regulatory Costs?

| Regulatory Cost | Volume Impact | SBI's Response | Net Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| NIM compression (10-20bps) | None — demand for credit is rate-elastic, not NIM-elastic | Grow volumes to offset margin compression | Flat to mildly negative |
| Higher provisioning norms | None — regulatory, not demand-driven | Absorb through PPOP buffer | Earnings impact but manageable |
| Capital adequacy tightening | Constrains growth if CRAR is thin | Occasional equity raise (QIP) | Minor dilution |

### Buffett's Three-Lens Framework:

**1. History as a Guide:** SBI survived the AQR (2015-16), demonetisation (2016), farm loan waivers (2017-19), and COVID (2020-21). Each time, the franchise emerged intact. The bank's sheer scale and government backing mean it absorbs regulatory shocks that would cripple smaller institutions. Recovery took 2-3 years from the worst of the NPA cycle.

**2. The Elasticity / Pricing Power Argument:** Banking is a spread business — SBI's pricing power comes from its cheap deposit base, not from customer willingness to pay higher rates. Regulatory costs (higher provisioning, PSL requirements) are absorbed through operating efficiency and volume growth, not pricing power. SBI's scale is its pricing moat.

**3. The Terminal Risk Assessment:** Is there any realistic scenario where regulation permanently destroys SBI? **No.** As a D-SIFI (Domestic Systemically Important Financial Institution) with implicit sovereign guarantee, SBI is literally too big to fail. The terminal risk probability is negligible. Even nationalisation is moot — SBI is already government-owned.

> *"It's only when the tide goes out that you learn who's been swimming naked."* — Warren Buffett. SBI was "swimming naked" during the NPA cycle, and the tide went out hard. But the bank survived, cleaned up, and emerged stronger. The regulatory framework that caused the pain (AQR, IBC) actually STRENGTHENED SBI's moat by forcing competitors to recognise bad loans too, while SBI's scale gave it the capital buffer to survive.
