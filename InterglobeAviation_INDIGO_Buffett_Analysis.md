## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

### Business Model

Interglobe Aviation, operating as **IndiGo**, is India's largest airline by market share (~64% domestic, ~21% international). The business model is deceptively simple: move people from Point A to Point B at the lowest possible cost, on time, without frills. IndiGo operates as a **low-cost carrier (LCC)** — it sells seats, not experiences. Passengers pay low base fares and optionally add ancillary services (meals, baggage, seats, insurance).

**Would Buffett understand it?** Absolutely — it is a simple transportation business. However, Buffett has historically **disliked airlines intensely.** His famous quote:

> *"If a farsighted capitalist had been present at Kitty Hawk, he would have done his friend a huge favor by shooting Orville down."*

**Connecting Buffett's airline scepticism to IndiGo's actual numbers:** IndiGo's operating history validates exactly the concerns Buffett identified. EBIT Margin has swung from **+16.5% (FY16)** to **-32.1% (FY21 COVID)** to **+14.4% (FY24)** and back to a **₹2,614 Cr single-quarter loss (Sep25)** — a ~49 percentage-point peak-to-trough range across cycles, using EBIT Margin consistently (FY24 OPM pre-D&A was 24%, but EBIT Margin after the surging D&A from fleet expansion was 14.4%). Even the cumulative ₹6,171 Cr net loss across FY20-FY22 (COVID) was not the company's first crisis: FY19 produced near-zero EBIT under fuel price stress. Buffett's framework — *"the worst sort of business is one that grows rapidly, requires significant capital to engender the growth, then earns little or no money"* — describes IndiGo's FY20-FY23 period precisely (₹17,000+ Cr capacity addition while net profit was negative). The current FY24-FY25 peak is therefore best understood as a cyclical high point against this volatile baseline, not a sustainable run-rate.

He later softened, buying a basket of airline stocks in 2016-2017 (Delta, Southwest, United, American) — only to sell them all at a loss in 2020 during COVID. His reasoning: *"The world has changed for airlines. I don't know how it's changed. I hope it corrects itself in a reasonably prompt way."*

**Business Classification:**
IndiGo is a **capacity-constrained commodity business with some cost advantages.** It is NOT a toll bridge (passengers have alternatives), NOT a consumer franchise (no brand loyalty at the margin — passengers buy on price and schedule), and NOT a switching-cost business (zero switching costs). However, IndiGo has built **structural cost advantages** through fleet commonality (single-type Airbus fleet reducing maintenance complexity), sale-and-leaseback financial engineering, and operational scale that competitors cannot easily replicate.

**The Economic Engine:**
- Revenue = Passengers × Fare per Passenger + Ancillary Revenue
- Costs are dominated by: **Aviation Turbine Fuel (~35-40% of costs)**, aircraft lease rentals, airport charges, employee costs, and maintenance
- The business is a **high-fixed-cost, low-margin, high-volume machine** — tiny changes in load factor or fuel prices create massive profit swings
- IndiGo's edge: lowest Cost Per Available Seat Kilometer (CASK) in India, driven by fleet efficiency, high aircraft utilisation, and scale economies

**Key Statistics (as of FY26):**
- Fleet: 440+ aircraft (7th largest airline globally by daily departures)
- Daily flights: ~2,200+
- Destinations: 95+ domestic, 40+ international
- Passengers carried CY25: 124 million
- Market share: ~64% domestic

### Trend Narrative

IndiGo's revenue trajectory tells two stories: a powerful underlying growth engine (Sales CAGR of ~19% over FY15-FY25 per Screener) disrupted by catastrophic external shocks (COVID in FY21). Revenue collapsed 59% in FY21, then recovered with explosive 110% growth in FY23 as pent-up travel demand collided with reduced industry capacity (Jet Airways and GoFirst bankruptcies).

**Margin story is the critical concern for a Buffett investor.** Operating margins have swung from +20% (FY16) to -1% (FY19) to +24% (FY24) — this is extreme cyclicality. The FY24-FY25 margin expansion to 22-24% was driven by a perfect storm of factors: low fuel prices, high load factors, reduced competition (GoFirst grounded), and post-COVID fare discipline. Whether these margins are sustainable or peak-cycle is THE key investment question.

Buffett: *"When a management team with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."* Airlines have notoriously bad economics. IndiGo's management is brilliant — but is the business economics genuinely different this time?

### Critical IndAS 116 Context

**The balance sheet looks terrifying — but it is an accounting illusion.** Pre-FY20, IndiGo's borrowings were ₹2,429 Cr with D/E of 0.35. Post-FY20, borrowings exploded to ₹22,719 Cr — NOT because the company went on a borrowing spree, but because **IndAS 116 required capitalisation of all operating leases on the balance sheet.** The ~₹67,000 Cr "borrowings" in FY25 are overwhelmingly **aircraft lease liabilities** — the exact same leases that existed before, just now visible.

**True financial position assessment:**
- **Cash & Investments:** IndiGo held ~₹26,400 Cr in investments (as of March 2025 balance sheet) plus cash equivalents within other assets. For reference, per the Q1 FY25 earnings call (June 2024), total cash was ₹36,100 Cr (₹22,088 Cr free cash + ₹14,013 Cr restricted); the March 2025 figure would be higher given strong cash generation in FY25. This is a **massive cash pile** for an airline.
- **True "Net Debt" excluding leases:** Minimal. IndiGo's traditional debt is negligible — the company has historically operated a sale-and-leaseback model with Airbus, meaning it doesn't own most of its aircraft.
- **Negative Equity Period (FY22-FY23):** Accumulated losses during COVID wiped out equity entirely. This was NOT a solvency crisis — the company never had any difficulty meeting its obligations because of strong cash generation from advance ticket sales and the sale-and-leaseback model.

**Buffett's Financial Fortress Assessment:**
The balance sheet is paradoxically both weaker and stronger than it appears. Weaker because the massive lease obligations are real commitments. Stronger because the actual cash position is robust, and IndiGo's working capital is structurally negative (customers pay BEFORE flying — working capital days of -123), meaning growth GENERATES cash rather than consuming it. This is one of IndiGo's most important economic characteristics.

### Cash Flow Quality Assessment

IndiGo's cash flow profile is remarkable for an airline. The CFO/Operating Profit ratio (per Screener) generally exceeds 100%, suggesting the company converts operating profit to cash at favourable rates. This is driven by the **negative working capital cycle** — IndiGo collects cash from passengers weeks before they fly, creating a float that grows with revenue.

FY24-FY25 FCF of ₹20,780 and ₹22,846 Cr represent FCF margins of 30% and 28% — extraordinary for any business, let alone an airline. At ₹592/share FCF (FY25), the stock trades at ~7.7x FCF on FY25 earnings — far cheaper than it appears on a P/E basis.

**Quantifying the IndAS 116 FCF inflation:** Under IndAS 116, principal portions of aircraft lease payments are reclassified from Operating Cash Flow (where they sat as rent in operating expenses pre-FY20) to Financing Cash Flow (as lease liability repayments). This artificially boosts reported CFO and therefore FCF. **Magnitude check:** IndiGo's annual lease liability repayments approximate ₹8,000-10,000 Cr/year based on the trajectory of borrowings movements net of new lease additions. If these were treated as operating outflows (the pre-IndAS framework), FCF would be approximately ₹12,846-14,846 Cr (vs. reported ₹22,846 Cr) — roughly **35-44% lower** (35% at ₹8,000 Cr lease assumption; 44% at ₹10,000 Cr). The reported FCF is therefore optically attractive but overstates true cash available to equity holders by a significant margin. **This is critical context for any FCF-based valuation multiple.**

**However, a critical caveat:** Under IndAS 116, lease payments that were previously operating expenses (in the P&L) are now classified as financing cash outflows (repayment of lease liabilities). This artificially INFLATES CFO and FCF. The true "cash available to the owner" after all lease obligations is lower than reported FCF. For a more honest comparison, EBITDAR minus cash lease rentals is a better proxy — which is essentially what the company reports as operating cash flow before IndAS 116 adjustments.

Buffett: *"Accounting is the language of business. If you don't understand it, you'll be relying on people who may or may not be telling you the truth."* IndAS 116 doesn't change IndiGo's economic reality — but it makes the numbers harder to interpret.

> **Note on ROIC:** Buffett cares deeply about returns on incremental invested capital, but he evaluates this intuitively from the business economics (can the company reinvest retained earnings at high rates?) rather than from a formulaic ROIC calculation. Formulaic ROIC can be distorted by goodwill treatment, lease capitalisation, and other accounting choices. The Owner Earnings framework in Section 8 captures this more honestly. Use ROIC as a directional signal, not a precise score.

> *"The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow, but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage."* — Warren Buffett (Fortune, 1999)

**Connection to IndiGo:** This quote is the central tension in IndiGo's investment thesis. India's aviation industry IS growing (a fact baked into every analyst model), but the question Buffett would ask is: *does IndiGo's cost advantage have durability?* The ratios above show the moat is real (ROCE 17-25% in good years, FCF margins 28-30%, lowest CASK in India) but its durability is questioned by the EBIT Margin volatility — the moat is too narrow to absorb cyclical shocks without being temporarily breached.

IndiGo passes Buffett's quality test on growth and competitive position, but **fails spectacularly on consistency.** The airline's earnings are wildly volatile — profits swing from +₹8,168 Cr to -₹6,171 Cr within a few years. This is fundamentally at odds with Buffett's preference for predictable compounders. The ratios reveal that IndiGo's moat is real (cost advantage, scale) but fragile — it can be destroyed overnight by fuel spikes, pandemics, or geopolitical events.

**Notes on CapEx:**
- IndiGo's traditional CapEx is very low (₹400-1,200 Cr/year) because the airline LEASES rather than owns its aircraft. The major "capital" commitments are the lease obligations, which under IndAS 116 show up as D&A and interest, not CapEx.
- Maintenance CapEx is estimated at ~50% of reported CapEx, with the rest being growth-related (technology, ground infrastructure).
- D&A (₹8,637 Cr in FY25) is dominated by Right-of-Use asset depreciation (aircraft leases) — this is the airline's true "cost of capital employed."

**Owner Earnings Assessment:** Normalised Owner Earnings (avg FY24-FY25) are approximately ₹14,826 Cr. This is the Buffett "true earning power" figure — what IndiGo generates for owners after maintaining the business.

**⚠️ CRITICAL CAVEAT — IndAS 116 Inflates Owner Earnings:**

The OE formula as applied here adds back the full ₹8,637 Cr of D&A while deducting only ₹610 Cr of maintenance CapEx. This creates a logical inconsistency with the document's own narrative (which states D&A is "the airline's true cost of capital employed"). Under IndAS 116:

- The bulk of D&A (~95%) is RoU asset amortisation — economically equivalent to aircraft lease costs
- These lease costs flow through P&L as D&A + interest, NOT as cash CapEx
- Adding back all D&A to compute OE treats the lease-cost benefit (NI is depressed by D&A) without acknowledging the corresponding cash outflow (lease payments flow through financing activities)
- Section 4 caveats this for FCF (~35-44% inflation); **the same caveat applies to OE**

**Magnitude check:** Annual aircraft lease cash payments approximate ₹8,000-10,000 Cr. If these were included as a "true" capital cost in OE, sustainable Owner Earnings would be in the range of **₹5,280-7,280 Cr** — not ₹14,826 Cr. **The reported OE of ₹14,826 Cr likely overstates sustainable economic earnings by 52-65%.**

| Lease cash assumption | Implied true sustainable OE | Overstatement vs ₹15,280 Cr reported (FY25)* |
|---|---|---|
| ₹8,000 Cr/year (low end) | ₹7,280 Cr | 52% |
| ₹9,000 Cr/year (mid) | ₹6,280 Cr | 59% |
| ₹10,000 Cr/year (high end) | ₹5,280 Cr | 65% |

*Benchmark uses FY25-specific OE of ₹15,280 Cr; against the FY24-FY25 normalised OE of ₹14,826 Cr used elsewhere in valuation, the overstatement is broadly similar at ~54-67% — the directional conclusion is the same.

**Important — no double-count of SBC:** GAAP Net Income already includes Stock-Based Compensation as an expense (recognised at grant-date fair value). The Owner Earnings formula used here (NI + D&A − Maint.CapEx) therefore does NOT subtract SBC again — doing so would double-count. The Dilution-Adjusted Owner Earnings variant (which subtracts cash spent on anti-dilution buybacks) is not applied to IndiGo because dilution is immaterial (<0.01% annually).

**Implication for valuation:** This is the central reason the DCF analysis in Section 15 includes a "Genuine Conservative" scenario using cycle-average OE of ₹11,390 Cr (which partially incorporates this haircut via the inclusion of FY23's recovery-year OE of ₹4,518 Cr). The Base case using ₹14,826 Cr should be interpreted as "if IndiGo recovers to recent peak earnings AND the IndAS 116 lease accounting is treated favourably." For a strict Buffett-conservative valuation, the Genuine Conservative scenario is the more defensible anchor.

**SBC is negligible** for IndiGo. ESOP allotments are tiny (200-34,000 shares at a time on a base of 38.66 Cr shares, equating to <0.01% annual dilution), meaning dilution is immaterial. Total SBC expense is typically <₹50 Cr/year (<0.7% of FY25 NI). No anti-dilution buyback adjustment is needed — this simplifies the Owner Earnings calculation.

## 10. COMPETITIVE POSITION & MOAT ANALYSIS

### Moat Assessment

**Moat Type: Cost Advantage + Scale Economies + Emerging Brand**

IndiGo's competitive position in Indian aviation is **dominant but not impregnable.** The moat is built on:

**1. Cost Leadership — The Primary Moat (Strong)**
IndiGo has the lowest CASK (Cost per Available Seat Kilometer) among Indian carriers. This is achieved through: single aircraft type (Airbus A320 family — reduces training, maintenance, and spare parts complexity); young fleet (fuel-efficient NEO engines); high aircraft utilisation (~12 hours/day); lean operations with low overheads. This cost advantage has been consistent for over a decade.

**2. Scale Economies (Strong)**
With 64% domestic market share and 440+ aircraft, IndiGo benefits from bulk purchasing (fuel, spare parts, airport services), negotiating power with airports and OEMs, brand recognition driving direct bookings (lower distribution costs), and network density enabling connections without traditional hub-and-spoke.

**3. Brand Recognition (Moderate)**
IndiGo = reliable, on-time, affordable. Among Indian consumers, it is the default choice. However, brand loyalty in airlines is thin — a ₹500 fare difference will shift passengers.

**4. Regulatory Moat (Emerging)**
Indian aviation has brutally high barriers to entry: airport slot scarcity, capital requirements, licensing complexity, and an operating environment that has bankrupted Kingfisher, Jet Airways, GoFirst, and multiple others. IndiGo is the last profitable large-scale survivor. Its ability to access scarce airport slots (especially at congested metros) is a growing structural advantage.

### Key Competitors

| Competitor | Threat Level | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| **Air India (Tata Group)** | High | Full-service carrier with Tata backing; international expansion; but years away from profitability |
| **SpiceJet** | Low | Financially distressed, shrinking fleet, survival uncertain |
| **Akasa Air** | Medium | Well-funded (Jhunjhunwala/Jio-backed), growing fast, modern fleet; but tiny scale |
| **Vistara (merged into Air India)** | N/A | Merged into Air India |

### Moat Durability & Change Risk

Buffett says: *"We're looking for the absence of change to protect ways that are already making a lot of money."*

**Change risk for IndiGo is HIGH (revised upward from MODERATE).** The airline industry is fundamentally susceptible to change — fuel prices, regulations, technology (electric/hydrogen aircraft decades away), competition, and consumer behaviour all shift. However, the core model of moving people by air is enduring and growing in India. The specific question is whether IndiGo's cost advantage and scale are durable enough to sustain above-industry-average profitability.

**⚠️ Self-Inflicted Moat Erosion: The A350 Widebody Decision**

IndiGo's own management is voluntarily introducing the exact type of change that would erode the primary moat. **The A350 widebody is NOT an A320 family derivative** — it requires:
- Separate pilot type ratings (no A320 pilot can fly A350 without ~6 months retraining)
- Separate maintenance teams, hangars, and tooling
- Separate spare parts inventory (Rolls-Royce Trent XWB engines vs. CFM LEAP-1A / P&W PW1100G on A320neo)
- Separate crew rosters and rest planning

This **directly negates Moat Point #1 (single-type-fleet cost leadership)** which Section 10 cites as IndiGo's primary moat. The transition from a single-type fleet to a two-type operator structurally raises CASK in the international segment and dilutes the cost advantage that has defined the business for a decade.

**Buffett would view this as:** Management *deliberately* changing what was working — the opposite of his "absence of change" preference. The international expansion thesis (higher-yield long-haul passengers) may justify the strategic shift, but it should be priced as a moat-narrowing event, not a value-additive one.

**Honest assessment:** IndiGo's moat is REAL but NARROWING. It is a cost moat, not a pricing power moat. The company cannot raise fares significantly above competitors — it wins by being the lowest cost producer in a commodity service. This is more akin to Walmart than Coca-Cola. The moat widened as competitors exited (Jet, GoFirst), but it is being actively eroded by:
1. **Self-inflicted complexity** (A350 widebody breaks single-fleet cost moat)
2. **Air India revival** under Tata (re-equipping, new aircraft, dropping costs)
3. **Akasa scaling** (well-funded competitor with modern fleet)

The moat can narrow significantly over the next 5-7 years. This is a **major reason** the strict Buffett verdict in Section 19 is PASS.

This is an **exceptional** $1 Test result — among the best for any Indian company. However, it is heavily influenced by the IPO pricing (relatively low in 2015) and subsequent re-rating. A meaningful portion of the "market value creation" came from multiple expansion in addition to earnings growth.

The capital allocation discipline is real, but the re-rating tailwind is unlikely to repeat from current ~24x PE — future per-rupee value creation will depend almost entirely on earnings compounding, since there is limited headroom for further multiple expansion in airline economics.

### Management Assessment

**Rahul Bhatia (Promoter & Chairman):** The architect of IndiGo's cost-discipline culture. Has been a steady hand through multiple industry crises. His decision to bet on a single-type fleet and sale-leaseback model was visionary.

**CEO Transition Risk:** Pieter Elbers (ex-KLM) departed March 2026 following operational disruptions. Willie Walsh (ex-CEO of British Airways and IAG, recently IATA Director General) is incoming by August 2026. Walsh is arguably the most experienced aviation CEO globally — his appointment is a positive signal for IndiGo's international ambitions. However, the interim period creates execution risk.

**Capital Allocation:** IndiGo has been conservative — no acquisitions, minimal dividends during growth, and the sale-leaseback model preserves capital. The company initiated its first dividend in 5 years in FY25 (5% payout).

**Concern: Promoter Selling.** The steady reduction from 68% to 42% is the most significant governance concern. While partly explained by the Gangwal family exit and portfolio diversification, it undermines the "skin in the game" thesis that Buffett prioritises.

**Management Candour:** IndiGo's investor communications are generally direct and transparent — earnings calls candidly discuss operational challenges (Pratt & Whitney groundings, FDTL impact, geopolitical fare disruptions). The Sep25 quarter loss was disclosed promptly with detailed root-cause analysis. Management does not engage in "non-GAAP" earnings reframing or aggressive accounting choices. The reporting under IndAS 116 is technical but accurately reflects the lease-heavy economics. However, there is room for improvement in disclosing forward-looking guidance — IndiGo has historically been conservative in providing earnings or capacity guidance, which is partly cultural (Indian listed companies generally guide less than US peers) and partly prudent given airline volatility. Buffett would value the direct disclosure of bad news but might prefer more forward guidance to assess management's own confidence in their plans.

**Insider Ownership Track:** Promoter holding at 41.57% remains meaningful but the declining trajectory (-26 pp in 3 years) is the dominant signal. The Gangwal family's exit is essentially complete; the Bhatia family (active management) retains a meaningful stake. Going forward, the question is whether Bhatia's residual holding stays put.

But the spread is compressing in FY25-FY26 due to fuel price and capacity disruption. Whether the moat holds against Air India's revival and the A350 transition is the key 3-5 year question.

**Important Caveats:**
1. DCF for airlines is inherently unreliable due to earnings volatility. A single bad year (fuel spike, pandemic) can destroy the terminal value assumptions.
2. **Conservative methodology note:** Owner Earnings (Net Income + D&A − Maintenance CapEx) is a *levered* cash flow — it is computed after interest expense (₹5,133 Cr in FY25, mostly lease interest). Standard DCF practice would either (a) use unlevered cash flows and subtract net debt, or (b) use levered cash flows and treat the result as equity value directly without subtracting debt. This analysis subtracts net debt from levered cash flows, which double-penalises for lease costs and **understates intrinsic value.** The DCF outputs in the scenario table above are therefore a conservative lower bound — actual intrinsic values are likely higher. **Quantified alternative (no double-count):** if net debt is NOT subtracted (treating OE as equity-level cash flow directly), the IVs shift as follows: Genuine Conservative IV/share rises from ₹4,511 to approximately ₹5,564 (MoS shifts from -1.2% to +18%); Base (Recovery) IV rises from ₹8,107 to approximately ₹9,160 (MoS rises from 43.7% to +50%). 3. **OE add-back concern:** The OE formula adds back the full ₹8,637 Cr D&A while deducting only ₹610 Cr maintenance CapEx. Under IndAS 116, the bulk of D&A is RoU asset amortisation — economically equivalent to aircraft lease cost. Section 4 caveats that FCF is inflated by IndAS 116 lease reclassification, and the same caveat applies to OE. The "true" cash available to equity (after meeting all lease obligations) is lower than reported OE. **For DCF purposes, the Base case using ₹14,826 Cr should be viewed with this caveat in mind** — per Section 8's magnitude analysis, the figure may overstate sustainable cash generation by 52-65% (using lease cash payments of ₹8,000-10,000 Cr/year). True sustainable OE is approximately ₹5,280-7,280 Cr.
4. **The Genuine Conservative scenario is the honest floor:** At ₹4,511 IV with cycle-average OE and decelerated growth, the stock at ₹4,565 has NO margin of safety. The bull case for IndiGo requires confidence that current headwinds are temporary and the FY24-FY25 earnings peak is achievable again. **This is the central risk question for the investment.**

The DCF range is wide (₹4,511 to ₹16,419) reflecting genuine uncertainty about IndiGo's cycle position. The Base case IV of ₹8,107 should be interpreted as the value IF the business recovers to recent peak earnings, not as a "conservative floor."

## 21. REGULATORY RISKS & POLICY HEADWINDS

### Key Regulatory Framework

IndiGo operates under the regulatory oversight of the **DGCA (Directorate General of Civil Aviation)**, Ministry of Civil Aviation (MoCA), and the Airports Authority of India (AAI). The regulatory stance is **broadly supportive** — the Indian government has identified aviation as a growth priority (UDAAN scheme, new airport construction, increased capacity).

### Current Headwinds

1. **FDTL (Flight Duty Time Limitation) Norms:** New DGCA norms implemented in 2025 limit pilot duty hours, constraining capacity growth. IndiGo experienced significant flight cancellations in Dec 2025 due to FDTL implementation. This is a structural constraint that limits near-term fleet utilisation.

2. **ATF Taxation:** Aviation Turbine Fuel is currently **excluded from GST** and is instead taxed via central excise duty plus state-level VAT (which varies from 1-30% across states), making Indian fuel costs among the highest globally. The industry has long lobbied for ATF's inclusion under GST, which would standardise and likely reduce the tax burden. This remains an ongoing structural headwind.

3. **Route Dispersal Guidelines:** Airlines are required to fly to unprofitable Tier 2/3 cities as a proportion of total routes. This constrains route optimisation.

### Historical Regulatory Impact

| Event | Year | Nature of Regulation | Impact on IndiGo |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5/20 Rule (abolished 2016) | Pre-2016 | Required 5 years of domestic ops + 20 aircraft before international flights | Delayed international expansion; abolished after lobbying — now a non-issue |
| Demonetisation | 2016 | Currency ban → temporary demand destruction | Q3 FY17 volume dip of ~5%; recovered within 1 quarter |
| GST implementation | 2017 | Service tax replaced by GST on airfares | Neutral — fares already under service tax regime |
| FDTL norms tightened | 2025 | Stricter pilot duty time limits → capacity constraints | Estimated 3-5% reduction in fleet utilisation; ~500 flight cancellations in Dec 2025 |
| Pratt & Whitney engine directive | 2023-25 | DGCA grounding of PW1100G-powered NEOs | 70+ aircraft grounded at peak; IndiGo's fleet diversification limited impact |

The track record shows IndiGo has **absorbed every regulatory disruption** without lasting damage. Recovery timelines have been 1-3 quarters at most.

### Demand Curve — Regulatory Cost Sensitivity

**1. History as a Guide:** IndiGo has survived demonetisation, COVID, engine groundings, and oil shocks — emerging stronger each time (gaining market share as weaker competitors folded). The business has a PROVEN track record of absorbing regulatory disruptions. Buffett would find this reassuring.

IndiGo can pass through 60-80% of cost increases via fares, with volume loss of only 2-5%. This is not Coca-Cola-level pricing power, but it is significantly better than a pure commodity business.

Nationalisation risk: negligible (India moved away from state-owned aviation decades ago). Product ban: impossible — air travel is essential infrastructure. Technology obsolescence: decades away. The terminal risk probability is **negligible.**
