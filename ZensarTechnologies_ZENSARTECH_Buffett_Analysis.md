## 1. THE BUSINESS — Understanding the Economic Engine

Zensar Technologies is a mid-cap Indian IT services company, part of the RPG Enterprises group (the same conglomerate behind CEAT Tyres and KEC International). Headquartered in Pune, Zensar provides digital solutions and technology services across two core segments: Digital and Application Services (DAS) and Digital Foundation Services (DFS). Its client verticals span Banking & Financial Services (BFSI), Hi-Tech & Manufacturing, Consumer Services, and Healthcare & Life Sciences, with offices in India, USA, UK, Europe, and Africa.

**Would Buffett understand this business?** Yes — at its core, Zensar sells human expertise (engineers, consultants, architects) to large global enterprises, primarily in the US, to help them build, maintain, and modernise their technology systems. It is a **labour-arbitrage-plus-expertise** business: Indian engineers deliver work at a fraction of the cost of onshore teams, while Zensar packages this with domain expertise and digital capabilities. This is a classic **project-based services business** with some annuity-like characteristics from long-term managed services contracts.

Buffett: *"Never invest in a business you cannot understand."* IT services is a straightforward model — you hire smart people, train them, deploy them on client projects, and bill for their time. The economics are simple: revenue = headcount × utilisation × billing rates. The question is whether Zensar has any durable advantage in this intensely competitive market.

**Business Type Classification:** Zensar is **neither a toll bridge nor a consumer franchise.** It is closer to a **switching-cost business** for its existing clients (clients embed Zensar teams deeply into their technology stack, making replacement costly and disruptive), but it operates in a **commodity-adjacent** market where differentiation is hard and pricing power is limited. The moat, if any, is narrow and client-relationship-based, not structural.

**Key FY26 Facts:**
- Revenue: $643.7M (₹5,687 Cr consolidated), growing 3.1% YoY in USD (7.7% in INR)
- Q4 FY26 order book: $401.8M — the largest quarter in company history (122.9% QoQ growth)
- Net cash position: $319.5M
- 85% of workforce AI-certified
- Largest deal in company history closed during Q4 FY26

### Trend Narrative

Revenue growth has been moderate and lumpy — the 27.6% spike in FY19 was driven by the acquisition of Keystone Logic/Foolproof, while the -9.6% decline in FY21 was the COVID impact. Organic growth has averaged 5-8% over most periods, significantly below top-tier Indian IT peers like Infosys (10-15%) or HCL Tech (12-15%). However, the profitability story is strong: net profit has grown at 2x the rate of revenue over the last 5 years, reflecting margin expansion from 7.6% EBIT Margin (FY23 trough) back to 13-15%. The FY24 profit spike (₹665 Cr, +102.7% YoY) was driven by a combination of margin recovery and provision write-backs/other income; FY25 showed a slight dip before FY26 resumed growth. EPS growth has outpaced revenue growth handsomely, indicating improving capital efficiency and operating leverage.

> **CRITICAL — Screener Data Note & Why EBITDA Is Not Used:**
> Screener.in's "Operating Profit" line item is calculated as Revenue minus all operating expenses BEFORE deducting Depreciation & Amortisation. This makes it **EBITDA-equivalent**, not true EBIT. This analysis deliberately does NOT track EBITDA as a standalone metric or use EBITDA-based valuation multiples. Buffett (2000 Letter): *"References to EBITDA make us shudder — does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?"* Munger: *"I think every time you see the word EBITDA, you should substitute the phrase 'bullshit earnings.'"* Instead, we always compute and track **EBIT** (Screener Operating Profit minus Depreciation) — the true operating profit after accounting for the capital the business consumes.

Buffett: *"In the business world, the rearview mirror is always clearer than the windshield."* The rear-view mirror shows a business that has grown slowly on the top line but managed margins well enough to deliver double-digit earnings compounding. The question is whether the windshield — the FY26 record order book of $401.8M — signals an acceleration in revenue growth.

### Financial Fortress Assessment

Zensar's balance sheet is a **financial fortress** by any standard. Key strengths:

1. **Virtually debt-free:** D/E has declined from 0.18 (FY14) to just 0.03 (FY25). Borrowings of ₹125 Cr against equity of ₹4,069 Cr is negligible. Debt/EBIT is a mere 0.17x — the company could repay all debt from ~2 months of operating profit. Interest coverage at 42.1x is fortress-grade.

2. **Strong net cash position:** Net Debt/Owner Earnings is deeply negative at -2.27x, confirming Zensar has more cash than it knows what to do with. The company had net cash of $319.5M (~₹2,700 Cr) at end of Q4 FY26 — this is almost 22% of the market cap sitting in cash!

3. **No goodwill concerns:** The acquisition of Keystone Logic (FY19) would have created some goodwill, but fixed assets have been declining since FY20 (from ₹1,334 Cr to ₹1,171 Cr), suggesting write-downs and amortisation have reduced intangible overhang.

4. **Book value compounding:** BVPS has compounded at approximately 14-15% CAGR over 10 years, from ₹43 to ₹181 — consistently building tangible shareholder wealth.

Buffett: *"We will reject interesting opportunities rather than over-leverage our balance sheet."* Zensar embodies this principle — it has grown steadily while maintaining a fortress balance sheet. The net cash position provides significant downside protection and optionality for acquisitions or capital returns.

> **Why Debt/EBIT and Net Debt/Owner Earnings instead of Debt/EBITDA:** EBITDA pretends the business has no capital consumption. Debt/EBIT and Net Debt/Owner Earnings tell you whether debt can be repaid from *real* economic earnings after maintaining the asset base. For Zensar, all debt metrics are deeply comfortable — the company is net cash regardless of which metric you use.

> **Note on ROIC:** Buffett cares deeply about returns on incremental invested capital, but he evaluates this intuitively from the business economics (can the company reinvest retained earnings at high rates?) rather than from a formulaic ROIC calculation. Formulaic ROIC can be distorted by goodwill treatment, lease capitalisation, and other accounting choices. The Owner Earnings framework in Section 8 captures this more honestly. Use ROIC as a directional signal, not a precise score.

Buffett on what these ratios actually measure: *"The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow, but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage. The products or services that have wide, sustainable moats around them are the ones that deliver rewards to investors."* Applied to Zensar's ratios: the consistently above-15% ROCE (22.8% 10-year average) and ROIC (13.7% 10-year average — both well above the 10-11% cost of capital) confirm a genuine competitive advantage exists — but the wide ROE range (11.6% to 25.2%) and EBIT margin volatility (7.6% to 15.1%) signal that this advantage is **narrow**, not wide. A wide-moat business (TCS, Infosys, or HUL in consumer staples) would show a much tighter range of returns through cycles. Zensar's returns are above-average but not bulletproof — exactly the profile of a "fair company at a wonderful price" (Sec 19) rather than a "wonderful company".

The ratio trends reveal a business with solid returns on capital (ROCE consistently above 15%), conservative financial management (debt approaching zero), and improving working capital efficiency (debtor days declining from 81 to 55). The key concern is the EBIT Margin volatility (7.6% to 15.1%) — even wider than the OPM range because D&A amplifies the swing. This reflects the project-based nature of IT services where margins can swing with utilisation, wage cycles, and client mix. Notably, OPM (pre-D&A) and EBIT Margin tell a consistent story for Zensar — both improved together as D&A has been declining, indicating the business is becoming more capital-light over time (a positive structural trend). The moat is narrow — Zensar is a mid-tier IT player without the brand power or scale of TCS, Infosys, or HCL Tech.

> **Bridging note — why the OE Summary uses 50% of D&A (~₹50 Cr) rather than 50% of reported CapEx (~₹15 Cr):** The two figures look contradictory but reflect a deliberate methodological choice. Reported CapEx for Indian IT services is artificially low because (a) leased offices/data centres are not capitalised under Indian GAAP, (b) cloud subscriptions and software tools are expensed rather than capitalised, and (c) laptops/workstations are often bought through operating leases. The reported D&A (₹102 Cr in FY25) is a more honest reflection of the actual annual asset consumption — it includes amortisation of historical capitalised assets, leasehold improvements, and acquisition-related intangibles (Keystone). For Buffett's Owner Earnings concept — *"the average annual amount of capitalised expenditures for plant and equipment, etc., that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume"* — D&A is a better proxy for true maintenance burden than reported CapEx. Using 50% of D&A (₹50 Cr) is the conservative central estimate; using 50% of reported CapEx (₹15 Cr) would overstate OE by ₹35 Cr (~5%) and is rejected as too optimistic. The 50% of D&A approach is used consistently throughout the document (Section 4 Owner Earnings table, Section 8 Owner Earnings Summary, Section 15 DCF starting point).

> **Justification for the 50% maintenance fraction itself:** For an IT services company like Zensar, total asset consumption (D&A) is dominated by (a) office infrastructure refurbishment, (b) laptops and workstations for delivery teams, (c) data centre equipment, and (d) software licences/tools. Of these, roughly half is genuine maintenance (replacing aged equipment, refreshing leased office space at end of lease cycles, replacing end-of-life laptops typically every 3-4 years) and half is growth-linked (capacity expansion for new client engagements, geographical expansion). A 50% split is reasonable for a steady-state, modest-growth IT services firm. For high-growth IT firms (>20% revenue growth), the growth share would be higher (60-70%). For Zensar at 5-8% growth, 50% is the appropriate central estimate. Note: even using 80% maintenance (more conservative — i.e., ₹80 Cr deducted), Owner Earnings would only fall to ~₹790 Cr — a ~4% change that does not materially affect the valuation conclusions.

## 10. COMPETITIVE POSITION & MOAT ANALYSIS

### Moat Type: **Narrow — Switching-Cost + Client-Relationship Based**

Zensar operates in a highly competitive market with several hundred Indian IT services companies vying for the same pool of global enterprise clients. The moat sources and threats are:

**Switching Costs (Moderate):** Once embedded in a client's technology stack, Zensar teams accumulate deep domain and system knowledge that is expensive and risky to replace. The average tenure of key client relationships exceeds 5-7 years. However, switching costs are **moderate, not high** — clients routinely multi-source their IT needs and can transition work to competitors over 6-12 months.

**Scale (Weak):** At $644M in revenue, Zensar is a fraction of TCS ($30B+), Infosys ($19B+), or even Mphasis ($1.7B). It lacks the scale to compete on the largest mega-deals and has limited pricing power against tier-1 competitors. However, its mid-tier size can be an advantage in agility and client intimacy for $10-100M deal sizes.

**Brand/Reputation (Weak):** The RPG Group backing provides some credibility, but Zensar's brand recognition among global CXOs is significantly below top-tier Indian IT firms.

**Competitors:** Direct competitors include Mphasis, LTTS, Persistent Systems, Coforge, NIIT Technologies, and Mastech — all mid-tier Indian IT companies. Existential threats come from (a) Tier-1 players (TCS, Infosys, Wipro) commoditising the mid-tier space, and (b) AI/automation potentially reducing the need for human engineers in application maintenance and testing — Zensar's bread-and-butter services.

**Market Share Trajectory: Modestly Eroding.** Zensar's USD revenue growth (FY26: 3.1% YoY, 1.7% in constant currency) is materially below the mid-tier Indian IT sector average of 8-12% — implying **Zensar is losing share, not gaining it**. Over FY21-FY26, Zensar grew USD revenue at roughly 6% CAGR while peers like Persistent (~20%), Coforge (~15%), and LTTS (~12%) grew substantially faster. Within the mid-tier Indian IT addressable market (~$50-80B), Zensar's ~1% share has likely compressed to ~0.8-0.9% over five years. The FY26 record order book of $401.8M is the first credible signal that this share erosion could reverse — but conversion to revenue (and back to share gain) is a 12-24 month story that has not yet shown up in the income statement. **Reader's framing: Zensar is currently a share-loser hoping to reverse via the order book; the investment thesis is partially predicated on this reversal happening.**

**Susceptibility to Change:** This is the critical concern. Buffett said: *"We are looking for the absence of change to protect ways that are already making a lot of money."* IT services is facing the **most significant structural change in its 30-year history** — generative AI and automation are threatening to fundamentally alter the labour-arbitrage model. If AI can write code, test code, and manage infrastructure 10x cheaper than human engineers, the IT services model is structurally impaired. Zensar's 85% AI-certified workforce is a defensive response, but the jury is very much out on whether mid-tier IT companies will be winners or losers in the AI transition.

**Moat Verdict:** Narrow and potentially narrowing. The business has client stickiness and decent returns on capital, but lacks the structural advantages (brand, scale, network effects) that would make it a wide-moat Buffett-quality franchise.
